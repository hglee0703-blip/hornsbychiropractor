Returning to Exercise Safely After Lower Back Pain

04 September 2026 · Lower back pain

Your back has settled enough that you are thinking about exercise again. Walking around the house feels manageable, but the idea of lifting weights, running or returning to Saturday sport still brings some uncertainty.

The answer is rarely to wait until you feel absolutely nothing. It is usually better to restart with a level of activity your back can tolerate, then build from there. Staying active is generally recommended for uncomplicated lower back pain, while prolonged bed rest can make recovery harder (healthdirect).

Check that it is appropriate to restart

Before focusing on sets, kilometres or training sessions, consider how your symptoms have behaved. Exercise is more reasonable when the pain is stable or improving, everyday movement is becoming easier, and you can complete basic tasks without a marked increase in symptoms afterwards.

Some symptoms need medical assessment rather than an exercise experiment. Seek urgent care if back pain is accompanied by new loss of bladder or bowel control, numbness around the genitals or buttocks, or significant weakness in the legs. These can be warning signs of serious nerve compression (healthdirect). Back pain following major trauma, or pain associated with fever, unexplained weight loss or a history of cancer, also warrants prompt assessment (Mayo Clinic).

Pain travelling into the leg deserves a little more care, particularly if it is accompanied by numbness or weakness. Sciatica can involve irritation or compression of a nerve supplying the leg (healthdirect). Our guide to disc protrusion, sciatica and treatment options covers this in more detail.

Begin below your previous training level

Your first session back is not a test. Choose an amount that feels almost too easy, especially if you have been inactive for several weeks. For walking, that might mean a short, level route. In the gym, it may mean fewer exercises, lighter resistance and longer rests. A runner could start with brisk walking or short walk-jog intervals rather than trying to match an old pace.

A useful approach is to change one thing at a time. Add a few minutes, a small amount of weight or another set, rather than increasing all of them during the same session. Exercise is supported as part of care for persistent lower back pain, although no single form of exercise is clearly best for every person (Cochrane review, 2021).

Use the following as a practical starting point:

  • Warm up with five to ten minutes of comfortable walking or easy cycling.
  • Keep the first few sessions shorter than your usual workout.
  • Leave at least a day between harder strength or running sessions.
  • Increase only if symptoms return to their usual level by the following day.
  • Reduce the next session if pain becomes progressively stronger or begins spreading further into the leg.

Judge the response, not just the pain during exercise

A brief ache during a squat, walk or mobility exercise does not automatically mean that you have caused further injury. Pain is influenced by several factors and does not always correspond directly with tissue damage (PubMed, 2017). Even so, pain should not be ignored. The location, intensity and pattern matter.

Mild, familiar discomfort that settles soon after activity may be acceptable. Stop and reassess if pain becomes sharp, keeps building, changes your movement substantially, or starts travelling further down the leg. New tingling, numbness or weakness also calls for professional assessment rather than pushing through.

The following morning is often more informative than the middle of the workout. If you are slightly stiff but moving normally, the dose was probably reasonable. If routine tasks are noticeably harder or symptoms remain clearly aggravated, scale back the duration, range of movement or load. This is not failure. It is simply information for the next session.

Build towards the activity you actually want to do

General movement is a useful base, but eventually your exercise needs to resemble your goal. Someone returning to gardening may need repeated bending, carrying and time on their feet. A gym-goer needs gradual exposure to loaded hinges, squats, pushes and pulls. Field sport adds running, acceleration and changes of direction.

Technique matters, but there is no need to make every movement rigid or fearful. Start with a range and load you can control, breathe normally, and avoid rushing tired repetitions. Strength and confidence can then be built through repeated, manageable exposure. If sitting, driving or leg symptoms remain limiting, our article on practical lumbar disc injury management discusses walking, posture, medication and McKenzie-style exercises.

A clinician can help if you are unsure what to modify, have repeated flare-ups, or cannot progress despite reducing the load. Assessment may include your symptoms, neurological function, movement and the demands of your chosen activity. Imaging is not routinely required for uncomplicated lower back pain, but may be considered where serious disease or significant nerve involvement is suspected (healthdirect).

This article provides general information only. It is not a diagnosis or individual treatment advice. Speak with a qualified health professional about your circumstances, particularly if symptoms are severe, worsening or associated with numbness or weakness.

Frequently asked questions

Should I wait until I am completely pain-free?

Not necessarily. For uncomplicated lower back pain, remaining active and gradually returning to normal activities is generally preferred to prolonged rest (healthdirect). Start at a manageable level and pay attention to how your symptoms behave during the following day.

Is walking a good first exercise?

Walking is often a practical starting point because the pace, distance and terrain are easy to adjust. Begin with a duration you can manage comfortably and build in small steps. Stop and seek advice if walking causes increasing leg pain, numbness or weakness.

Can I return to squats and deadlifts?

Often, yes, but the starting load and range may need to be reduced. Practise the movement with control, avoid training to fatigue at first, and increase one variable at a time. Individual assessment is sensible if loaded bending repeatedly causes spreading leg symptoms or marked pain.

When should I see a health professional?

Arrange an assessment if symptoms are worsening, keep returning, interfere with sleep or daily activity, or include leg numbness or weakness. New bladder or bowel problems, saddle-area numbness or significant leg weakness require urgent medical care (healthdirect).
