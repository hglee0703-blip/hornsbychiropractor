Standing Desk Setup to Prevent Back and Neck Pain

17 September 2026 · Neck & posture

Your new standing desk is assembled, the monitor is perched on top, and by mid-afternoon your neck feels tight. Perhaps your lower back is aching too. Simply raising a desk does not automatically make the workstation comfortable.

The aim is not to hold one ideal posture all day. A useful setup supports you while sitting and standing, keeps frequently used equipment within easy reach, and makes it simple to change position.

A standing desk is still a desk

Start with the work you actually do. If most of your day involves typing, adjust the desk around your keyboard and mouse rather than the monitor. Your shoulders should feel relaxed, your elbows should sit close to your sides, and your forearms should be roughly level while you type. Computer workstation guidance recommends keeping the keyboard and mouse at a height that avoids raised shoulders or excessive bending at the wrist (US Occupational Safety and Health Administration).

Stand close enough that you do not have to reach forwards. If your elbows drift away from your body every time you use the mouse, bring it closer. Documents, a phone and other regularly used items should also be within comfortable reach. This sounds minor, but repeated reaching can turn an otherwise reasonable setup into a tiring one.

If you use a laptop for more than brief tasks, consider a separate keyboard and mouse so the screen and keyboard can be positioned independently. A laptop alone usually forces a compromise: either the screen is low or the keyboard is too high. OSHA’s laptop workstation guidance also recommends using external equipment where practical for prolonged computer work (US Occupational Safety and Health Administration).

Put the screen in front of your eyes, not below them

Place the main monitor directly in front of you. As a starting point, the top of the visible screen can sit at or slightly below eye level, with the monitor about an arm’s length away. Screen size, eyesight and whether you wear multifocal glasses may change what feels comfortable. Ergonomic guidance supports placing the screen directly ahead and adjusting its height and distance to avoid awkward head and neck positions (US Occupational Safety and Health Administration).

Now look at the centre of the screen without deliberately pulling your chin back or lifting your chest. You should be able to read comfortably while keeping your head reasonably upright. Increase the text size if you are leaning forwards to read. If glare is making you crane your neck, change the screen angle or nearby lighting rather than trying to hold yourself rigidly.

For two monitors, put the one you use most directly in front. If both are used equally, place them close together and centre yourself between them. Turn with your chair, feet or whole body rather than repeatedly twisting only through the neck.

Stand comfortably rather than standing at attention

Once the desk and screen are set, let your weight settle through both feet. Keep your knees relaxed and avoid forcing your shoulders back. A small footrest can be useful because it gives you another position, but switch feet from time to time. Supportive footwear may be more comfortable than standing for hours in unsupportive shoes.

Watch for habits such as leaning heavily on one arm, locking one knee or resting all your weight through one hip. None of these positions needs to be treated as forbidden. The problem is staying there long enough to become uncomfortable. If you use an anti-fatigue mat, choose one that lies flat and does not create a trip hazard.

Do not assume discomfort means the desk must go higher. A desk that is too high often causes shoulder shrugging, while one that is too low may encourage you to bend forwards. Make small adjustments, then work normally for a while before changing it again. I generally find that checking the keyboard height first makes the rest of the setup easier.

Build standing time gradually and keep moving

A standing desk is best used as a way to vary your day, not as a replacement for all sitting. Research reviews suggest sit-stand desks can reduce workplace sitting in the short term, but the evidence about longer-term health effects remains limited or uncertain (Cochrane review via PubMed). Australian health guidance recommends reducing prolonged sitting and breaking it up regularly rather than relying on one fixed posture (healthdirect).

Begin with brief standing periods, perhaps while reading emails or taking a call, then sit before your legs or back become tired. Change position during natural gaps in the day. A short walk to refill your water, speak with a colleague or step outside at lunch gives your body a different task altogether.

A standing desk is not a treatment for every cause of back or neck pain. If sitting, driving or leg symptoms are part of the problem, this guide to practical lumbar disc injury management covers those activities in more detail. Pain travelling into the leg, numbness or weakness may need individual assessment; our article on disc protrusion and sciatica explains the available management options.

Frequently asked questions

How long should I stand at a standing desk?

There is no single standing time that suits everyone. Start with short periods and alternate between standing, sitting and walking before discomfort builds. Australian guidance recommends regularly breaking up prolonged sitting rather than replacing it with prolonged stationary standing (healthdirect).

Should my feet or lower back hurt while standing?

No workstation should require you to push through increasing pain. Check the desk height, footwear and how long you have been standing, then change position. Seek professional advice if discomfort persists, worsens or continues away from the desk.

Can a standing desk fix sciatica?

A standing desk does not fix sciatica by itself. Sciatica describes symptoms linked to irritation or compression of the sciatic nerve, and the appropriate care depends on the cause and severity (healthdirect). Arrange an assessment if you have persistent pain, numbness or weakness in the leg.

Where should I place two monitors?

Put the monitor used most often directly in front of you, with the second close beside it. If you use both equally, centre yourself between them and keep the screens at similar heights. Turn your whole body for longer tasks rather than holding your neck rotated towards one screen (US Occupational Safety and Health Administration).
