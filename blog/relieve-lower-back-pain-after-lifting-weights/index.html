Lower Back Pain After Lifting Weights

18 September 2026 · Lower back pain

You finish a heavy set, put the bar down and feel your lower back tighten as you stand. Sometimes the pain is immediate. At other times, it builds during the drive home or later that evening.

A painful back after lifting does not automatically mean that you have torn something or slipped a disc. Pain intensity alone cannot identify the tissue involved, and most episodes of uncomplicated lower back pain improve with sensible activity and time (healthdirect).

What to do straight after the pain starts

Stop the set and put the weight down safely. Avoid repeatedly bending, twisting or redoing the movement to check whether it still hurts. Give the area a few minutes to settle, then see whether you can walk comfortably and move your legs normally.

For the rest of the day, reduce movements that produce sharp or rapidly increasing pain. That does not usually mean lying down for hours. Australian guidance encourages people with uncomplicated acute lower back pain to remain active as tolerated and avoid prolonged bed rest (Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care).

A cold pack wrapped in a towel may feel useful soon after the aggravation, while some people prefer a warm shower or heat pack for muscle tightness. Either option can be used for short periods as long as the skin is protected; heat and cold are mainly for temporary symptom relief (Mayo Clinic).

Keep moving, but adjust the load

Short, easy walks are often a reasonable place to start. Change position regularly rather than staying on the couch, at a desk or behind the wheel for a long stretch. The aim is not to push through sharp pain. It is to keep ordinary movement going within a tolerable range.

You might find one direction easier than another. For example, standing and walking may feel better than sitting, or gentle bending may be more comfortable than arching backwards. Do not force a stretch simply because it is described as a back exercise online. Our guide to practical lumbar disc injury management discusses walking, sitting, driving and McKenzie-style movements, but an exercise should be chosen according to your symptoms rather than the name of a suspected injury.

If you are considering pain medicine, ask a pharmacist or GP what is suitable for you. Anti-inflammatory medicines are not appropriate for everyone, particularly people with certain kidney, stomach, heart, blood pressure or medication-related risks (healthdirect). Follow the label and do not combine products unless a health professional has checked their active ingredients.

Returning to squats, deadlifts and rows

Do not use the next heavy session as a test. A more cautious return starts once walking, getting dressed, sitting and basic bending are manageable without a marked flare-up. Begin with a much lighter load, fewer sets and an exercise variation you can control. Stop if pain becomes sharp, spreads further down the leg or changes your movement significantly.

Before adding plates, practise the movement with no load or a light implement. Keep the load close to your body, set your feet securely and create gentle trunk tension before lifting. Avoid rushing a fatigued repetition. There is no need to hold one rigid spinal position all day, but controlled technique becomes more relevant as the load and fatigue increase.

Build back over several sessions rather than trying to recover missed training in one workout. Regular exercise is part of recommended care for persistent lower back pain, although no single exercise type is clearly best for every person (Cochrane review via PubMed, 2021). Your return may need adjusting if symptoms continue between sessions or your normal strength and confidence do not return.

When the pain needs to be assessed

Seek urgent medical care if back pain is accompanied by new loss of bladder or bowel control, numbness around the genitals or buttocks, substantial leg weakness, difficulty walking, or symptoms after major trauma. These can indicate a serious problem requiring prompt assessment (healthdirect). Fever, unexplained weight loss, a history of cancer or feeling systemically unwell also warrant medical review rather than self-treatment (Mayo Clinic).

Arrange a non-urgent assessment if the pain is not beginning to settle, repeatedly returns with lifting, travels below the knee, or is associated with ongoing tingling, numbness or weakness. Sciatica usually refers to pain and related symptoms caused by irritation or compression affecting the sciatic nerve pathway (healthdirect). Our longer article on disc protrusion, sciatica, injections and surgery explains those terms and the usual treatment options in more detail.

Scans are not routinely required for a new episode of lower back pain unless the examination suggests a serious cause or the result would change management (Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care). Disc bulges and other age-related findings are also seen on scans of many people who have no back pain, so an image needs to be interpreted alongside the symptoms and physical examination (PubMed, 2015).

Frequently asked questions

These answers cover common practical concerns, but they cannot account for your individual medical history or examination findings.

Should I train again the next day?

Avoid another heavy session if ordinary movement is still painful or your technique changes to protect the back. Light walking or gentle, tolerable movement is usually preferable to prolonged bed rest for uncomplicated lower back pain (Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care). Return to loaded training gradually rather than testing your maximum strength.

Is heat or ice better after lifting?

Either may provide short-term comfort, and personal preference is reasonable. Use a towel between the pack and your skin, limit each application to a short period and stop if the skin becomes painful or unusually numb (Mayo Clinic).

Does pain after a deadlift mean I have a slipped disc?

No. Symptoms after a deadlift can arise from several structures, and the lifting movement alone cannot establish a diagnosis. Disc changes are commonly found on imaging in people without symptoms, so examination findings and the pattern of pain matter (PubMed, 2015).

What is the best sleeping position for a sore lower back?

Choose the position that lets you settle without increasing the pain. A pillow between the knees when side-lying, or beneath the knees when lying on your back, may reduce strain and improve comfort for some people (Mayo Clinic). Change position if you develop increasing leg pain, tingling or numbness.
