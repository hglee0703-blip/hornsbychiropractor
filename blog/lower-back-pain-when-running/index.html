Lower Back Pain When Running: Causes & Prevention

07 September 2026 · Lower back pain

The first few kilometres feel comfortable, then a dull ache starts across your lower back. Perhaps it disappears soon after the run. Or it may hang around when you sit at your desk, drive home or get out of bed the next morning.

There is rarely one explanation that fits every runner. The timing of the pain, recent changes in training and any symptoms travelling into the leg are often more useful than simply pointing to the sore spot.

Why can running bring on lower back pain?

Running repeatedly loads the legs, pelvis and spine. Your body usually adapts to that load, but a sudden change in distance, speed, hills or running frequency may exceed what the tissues are currently used to. Fatigue can also change running mechanics, although pain cannot reliably be blamed on one posture or movement pattern alone.

Running itself is not automatically harmful to the lumbar spine. One imaging study found that long-term endurance runners had signs of healthier lumbar intervertebral discs than people who did not regularly exercise, although the observational design cannot prove that running caused the difference (Scientific Reports, 2017). The more useful question is whether your present amount and style of running suit your current capacity.

Common sources of pain in runners

A muscle or ligament strain is one possibility, particularly after an awkward movement or an abrupt increase in physical demand. Strains are among the recognised causes of back pain, but symptoms alone do not always identify which tissue is involved (Mayo Clinic). Many episodes are described as non-specific low back pain because no single structure can be confidently named as the cause (healthdirect).

Other possibilities include irritation around a lumbar joint or disc. A disc problem may cause back pain alone, while irritation of a spinal nerve can produce pain, tingling, numbness or weakness into the buttock and leg (healthdirect). If sitting, driving or bending also aggravates your symptoms, our guide to practical lumbar disc management explains some everyday options. There is also a more detailed article about disc protrusion, sciatica, injections and surgery.

Running may simply expose a problem that is already irritable. Long periods of sitting, a recent lifting incident, poor sleep, illness or returning after time away from exercise can all be relevant background. That does not mean one of these factors must be the cause. Back pain is often influenced by several physical and personal factors rather than a single fault visible on a scan (healthdirect).

Clues that help decide what to do next

Notice when the discomfort starts, whether it changes your stride and how long it takes to settle. Pain that appears only after a particular distance may point towards a load or fatigue issue. Pain that is worsening from week to week, occurs during ordinary daily tasks or no longer settles between runs deserves a proper assessment.

Seek prompt medical attention if back pain follows significant trauma, or is accompanied by fever, unexplained weight loss, progressive leg weakness, loss of bladder or bowel control, numbness around the groin, or difficulty passing urine. These symptoms can indicate a condition requiring urgent investigation (healthdirect). Call triple zero if symptoms are severe or you believe the situation is an emergency.

Scans are not routinely needed for uncomplicated lower back pain. A health professional will usually begin with the history and physical examination, then consider imaging if there are warning signs or if the result is likely to change management (healthdirect).

Practical ways to reduce the chance of a flare-up

Start by making training changes gradually. Avoid increasing distance, pace, hills and running days all at once. After illness, injury or a long break, an easy run-walk approach may be more manageable than immediately repeating your old route. Keep at least some runs genuinely easy, and allow recovery time after a harder session.

General strength work can support a running programme. Australian physical activity guidelines recommend muscle-strengthening activities on at least two days each week for adults, adjusted for health and ability (Australian Department of Health). Exercises might include a squat or sit-to-stand, a hip hinge, calf raises and a comfortable trunk exercise. There is also evidence that exercise can help reduce recurrences of low back pain, although no single routine is right for everybody (JAMA Internal Medicine, 2016).

  • Warm up simply: begin with several minutes of walking or easy jogging before settling into your planned pace.
  • Check your route: repeated steep hills or heavily cambered roads may be worth reducing while symptoms are irritable.
  • Use comfortable shoes: replace footwear that is badly worn, but do not assume that buying a particular shoe will correct back pain.
  • Review your stride carefully: a modest increase in step rate can reduce some lower-limb joint loading in selected runners, but it should not be treated as a universal cure for back pain (Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise, 2011).

What should you do if your back already hurts?

If pain is mild, reduce the distance or switch temporarily to walking rather than repeatedly testing the same painful run. Stop if your stride becomes noticeably guarded, symptoms spread down the leg or the pain continues to build. Prolonged bed rest is generally discouraged for ordinary back pain, and gentle daily activity is usually preferred as tolerated (healthdirect).

Heat or cold may provide short-term comfort for some people. Pain medicines and anti-inflammatory medicines are not suitable for everyone, so check with a pharmacist or doctor, particularly if you have kidney, stomach, heart or medication-related concerns (healthdirect). An assessment can help distinguish a manageable training flare-up from a problem requiring a different approach.

Frequently asked questions

Can I keep running with lower back pain?

A shorter, easier run may be reasonable if discomfort is mild, does not alter your stride and settles promptly afterwards. Stop and seek advice if pain is increasing, travelling into the leg or accompanied by numbness or weakness (healthdirect).

Should I stretch my lower back before running?

Gentle movement may feel useful, but stretching is not a complete answer for every type of back pain. Exercise can help with low back pain, although research does not support one exercise style as best for every person (Cochrane, 2021).

Are my running shoes causing the pain?

Shoes can affect comfort and running mechanics, but lower back pain should not automatically be attributed to footwear. Consider shoe wear alongside training changes, recovery, daily activities and your symptoms rather than searching for one perfect model.

When should I book an assessment?

Arrange an assessment if pain keeps returning, worsens across several runs, affects sleep or daily activity, or includes leg symptoms. Seek urgent care for bladder or bowel changes, groin numbness, progressive weakness, fever or significant trauma (healthdirect).
