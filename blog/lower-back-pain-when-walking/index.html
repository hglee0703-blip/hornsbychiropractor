Why Does My Lower Back Hurt When Walking?

01 September 2026 · Lower back pain

You leave home feeling reasonably comfortable, but after ten minutes of walking an ache starts across your belt line. Perhaps it builds until you have to stop. For someone else, the first few steps are the worst and the back loosens as the walk continues.

Walking pain does not point to one particular diagnosis. Lower back pain can involve muscles, joints, discs or nerves, and often there is no single structure that fully explains it (healthdirect). The way the pain behaves gives us useful clues, but it needs to be considered alongside your health history and a physical examination.

The timing of the pain matters

Notice how far you can walk before the pain begins, whether it spreads into a buttock or leg, and what settles it. Pain that is worse during standing and walking but eases with sitting or leaning forward can occur with lumbar spinal stenosis, where space around the spinal nerves has narrowed (Mayo Clinic). That pattern is a clue, not a diagnosis.

If your back feels stiff at the start and becomes easier after a few minutes, the current walking load may simply be more than your back is ready for. A recent increase in distance, hills, speed or carrying weight is worth mentioning. Pain that becomes steadily worse despite reducing the load deserves assessment rather than repeated attempts to push through it.

Common reasons walking can stir up the lower back

Muscles and spinal joints have to manage repeated load with every step. A back that has recently been strained or has become sensitive after less activity may complain during a longer walk. Most episodes of lower back pain are described as non-specific, meaning they cannot be reliably attributed to one precise tissue (Cochrane).

A disc problem may cause local back pain, but it can also irritate a spinal nerve. Sciatica usually produces pain travelling through the buttock and down one leg, sometimes with tingling, numbness or weakness (healthdirect). Narrowing around the nerves is more common as the spine ages and may limit standing or walking tolerance (Mayo Clinic). Pain felt near the lower back can also come from the hip or another nearby area, so the location alone is not enough to identify its source.

What to do on your next walk

Do not treat the walk as a test you must pass. Try a flatter route, a slower pace or a shorter distance that does not leave you markedly worse afterwards. Brief rests can be sensible. Remaining active is generally recommended for uncomplicated lower back pain, while prolonged bed rest can slow recovery (healthdirect).

Supportive shoes may make walking more comfortable, but there is no single correct walking posture. Aim for a relaxed, natural stride rather than holding your stomach rigid or forcing your shoulders back. If leaning forward feels better, using a railing or shopping trolley briefly may help you judge your tolerance, though a strong and repeatable need to bend forward should be discussed with a clinician.

Exercise can help persistent non-specific lower back pain, but the suitable type and starting dose vary between people (Cochrane). If disc-related pain has been suggested, our practical guide to lumbar disc management covers walking, sitting, driving and commonly used exercises. Stop an exercise if it causes new leg weakness, spreading numbness or a substantial increase in symptoms.

Signs that should not wait

Seek urgent medical care if back pain is accompanied by new trouble controlling your bladder or bowel, numbness around the genitals or buttocks, or significant weakness in both legs. These can be signs of severe pressure on the nerves at the base of the spine and require prompt assessment (healthdirect).

Arrange medical review promptly after a serious fall or accident, or if the pain comes with fever, unexplained weight loss, abdominal throbbing, a history of cancer, or severe pain that does not improve with rest. Back pain can occasionally reflect a condition outside the usual muscles, joints and discs (Mayo Clinic). Call triple zero if symptoms are severe or you believe there is an emergency.

What an assessment may involve

I would want to know where the pain starts, how long you can walk, what stops it, and whether coughing, sitting or bending changes it. An examination may include spinal and hip movement, leg strength, reflexes, sensation and walking. These findings help decide whether the problem appears mechanical or needs medical investigation.

Scans are not routinely required for a new episode of uncomplicated lower back pain, because imaging findings may not identify the cause of symptoms and often do not change early care (healthdirect). Imaging or referral may be appropriate where there are warning signs, neurological changes, trauma, or symptoms that remain troublesome. Care should then be matched to the assessment rather than based on a scan report alone.

Frequently asked questions

These answers cover common concerns, but persistent or changing symptoms still need individual assessment.

Should I keep walking with lower back pain?

Gentle activity is usually preferred to prolonged bed rest for uncomplicated lower back pain (healthdirect). Shorten the route or slow down if pain keeps building, and seek advice if walking causes spreading numbness, weakness or a marked deterioration.

Why does leaning over a shopping trolley help?

Bending forward can create more space around the lumbar nerves, so it may ease walking symptoms associated with spinal stenosis (Mayo Clinic). It is a recognised pattern, but it cannot confirm the diagnosis by itself.

Does leg pain mean I have a slipped disc?

No. Sciatica describes symptoms from an irritated or compressed nerve and can have several causes, including a disc problem or spinal narrowing (healthdirect). The distribution of pain, sensation, strength and reflexes can help a clinician assess what is happening.

Do I need an X-ray or MRI?

Most new episodes of uncomplicated lower back pain do not require immediate imaging (healthdirect). A clinician may recommend imaging if there has been significant trauma, warning signs are present, neurological function is changing, or the result is likely to alter management.
