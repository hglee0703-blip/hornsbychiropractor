Jaw Tension and Headaches From Teeth Grinding

22 September 2026 · Jaw & headache

You wake with a tight jaw, tender temples and a headache that seems to sit across the side of your face. Breakfast feels uncomfortable, and you may notice yourself pressing your teeth together again while concentrating later that day.

This pattern can occur with bruxism, which is the clenching or grinding of teeth while awake or asleep. Jaw soreness, tooth sensitivity, worn teeth and a dull headache beginning around the temples are recognised symptoms (Mayo Clinic).

Is the headache really from grinding?

A morning headache alongside jaw stiffness makes grinding worth considering, but it does not prove that grinding is the cause. Headaches have many possible triggers, including dehydration, illness, migraine, medication use and problems involving the neck or eyes (healthdirect).

Sleep bruxism happens during sleep, so another person may hear it before you realise it is happening. Awake bruxism is often quieter and may look more like sustained clenching during work, driving or concentration (Mayo Clinic). Stress, frustration and intense concentration can be associated with awake clenching, although there is rarely one simple explanation for every person (Mayo Clinic).

Pain can also come from a temporomandibular disorder, often shortened to TMD. This broad term covers problems affecting the jaw joints, chewing muscles and related tissues, with symptoms that may include jaw pain, restricted movement and pain spreading into the face or neck (National Institute of Dental and Craniofacial Research).

What to do when the jaw feels tight

Start by reducing the amount of work your jaw is doing. These measures are deliberately gentle:

  • Check whether your teeth are touching. Give them a little space when you are not eating or swallowing rather than holding a clench. Reducing habits such as jaw clenching is part of conservative self-management for TMD symptoms (National Institute of Dental and Craniofacial Research).
  • Try warmth or cold. A wrapped warm pack or cold pack against the sore area may be used for short-term symptom relief, depending on which feels more comfortable (National Institute of Dental and Craniofacial Research). Do not place a very hot or frozen pack directly on the skin.
  • Keep movement comfortable. Slowly open and close the mouth within an easy range rather than forcing a stretch. Gentle jaw exercises may form part of conservative care, but exercises should be selected carefully if movement catches, locks or sharply increases pain (National Institute of Dental and Craniofacial Research).
  • Temporarily choose easier foods. Softer food and avoiding chewing gum can reduce demand on an irritated jaw while it settles (National Institute of Dental and Craniofacial Research).

Simple pain relief may be appropriate for some people, but anti-inflammatory medicines and paracetamol are not suitable for everyone. Check the label and speak with a pharmacist or doctor if you have other health conditions, take regular medicines, are pregnant or are unsure what is safe for you (healthdirect).

Avoid repeatedly testing the sore spot, opening as wide as possible or digging hard into the jaw muscles. More pressure is not necessarily better, especially when the area is already sensitive.

Reducing grinding at night and during the day

A dentist may recommend a splint or mouthguard to keep the upper and lower teeth separated and reduce tooth damage. A guard can protect the teeth, but it may not stop the underlying grinding behaviour (Mayo Clinic). Fit matters, so persistent symptoms, tooth wear or problems with an over-the-counter guard should be discussed with a dentist.

During the day, use ordinary routines as reminders to release your jaw. Checking while answering email, waiting at traffic lights or preparing a meal can help you notice a clench without constantly monitoring yourself. Behaviour change, relaxation and biofeedback may be considered for awake bruxism, although the most suitable approach varies (Mayo Clinic).

A regular sleep routine and time to wind down may also be useful. Mayo Clinic advises limiting alcohol and avoiding stimulating drinks such as caffeinated coffee in the evening as part of bruxism self-care (Mayo Clinic). These changes are not a guaranteed cure, but they are reasonable habits to review before chasing complicated treatments.

Who should assess jaw pain and headaches?

A dentist is the right starting point if there is tooth sensitivity, visible wear, cracked teeth, a change in the bite or concern about night grinding. Dental examination can identify signs of bruxism and assess whether a properly fitted mouthguard is appropriate (Mayo Clinic).

As a chiropractor, my role is narrower. I can assess how the jaw and neck move, look for muscle or joint tenderness and consider whether neck symptoms are accompanying the headache. Dental disease still needs a dentist, and a new, changing or persistent headache deserves medical assessment. Conservative and reversible care is generally preferred for TMD, while treatments that permanently alter the teeth or bite should be approached cautiously (National Institute of Dental and Craniofacial Research).

Call triple zero for a sudden severe headache or a headache with weakness, difficulty speaking, confusion, collapse or other signs of a medical emergency. Urgent assessment is also appropriate after a significant head injury, or when headache occurs with fever and neck stiffness (healthdirect).

Frequently asked questions

Can teeth grinding cause a morning headache?

Yes, a dull headache around the temples can occur with bruxism, particularly when it appears with jaw soreness or tired chewing muscles (Mayo Clinic). A morning headache can have other causes, so recurring or worsening symptoms should not automatically be blamed on the jaw.

Should I wear a mouthguard every night?

Use one if your dentist recommends it and follow their advice about fit, cleaning and review. Mouthguards can separate the teeth and help prevent damage, but they do not necessarily stop grinding itself (Mayo Clinic).

Does a clicking jaw mean the joint is damaged?

Not necessarily. Jaw sounds without pain are common and usually do not require treatment, while clicking with pain, locking or restricted opening deserves assessment (National Institute of Dental and Craniofacial Research).

Can chiropractic care stop teeth grinding?

Chiropractic care should not be presented as a cure for bruxism. Assessment may help clarify whether neck or jaw movement is contributing to your symptoms, but tooth damage and mouthguard decisions belong with a dentist, and persistent headaches may need a GP.
