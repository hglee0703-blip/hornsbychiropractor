Lower Back Pain on Long Train Commutes

12 September 2026 · Lower back pain

By the time a train from the city reaches Hornsby, a mildly uncomfortable lower back can feel stiff, compressed or simply fed up. The trip may involve an hour or more in one seat, often with a laptop bag at your feet and little room to move.

The aim is not to hold one supposedly perfect posture for the entire journey. A more realistic approach is to make the seat tolerable, change position regularly and build a little movement into either side of the commute.

Why the train ride may stir up your back

Lower back pain can have several contributing factors, and often cannot be traced to one specific joint, muscle or disc (Better Health Channel). If sitting is already an aggravating activity, a long train trip gives you few natural breaks from it.

Notice where the symptoms go. An ache that remains around the belt line is different from pain, tingling, numbness or weakness travelling into the buttock and leg. Those leg symptoms can occur with sciatica, which involves irritation or compression affecting the sciatic nerve pathway (Mayo Clinic). They deserve assessment if they are new, persistent or worsening.

Set up the seat without becoming rigid

Start with both feet supported rather than tucked beneath the seat or perched on a bag. Let your knees rest in a comfortable position and sit far enough back that the backrest can take some of your weight. General posture guidance supports using comfortable, supported positions rather than placing unnecessary strain on the body (Better Health Channel).

If the seat feels hollow behind your waist, try a small folded jumper or rolled hand towel as temporary support. Keep it modest. A large, firm roll can push you too far forward and may feel worse. Treat it as an experiment: keep it if the back settles, remove it if symptoms increase or begin travelling further into the leg.

Avoid bracing your abdominal muscles for the whole journey. Instead, change between a few comfortable positions. You might sit back for several stations, lean forward briefly with your forearms supported on your thighs, then return to the backrest. Use station stops as reminders to uncross your legs, reposition your feet or gently move your pelvis.

Use small amounts of movement during the commute

A practical sequence I often suggest is simple: walk along the platform before boarding, move occasionally during the trip, then walk again after getting off. Exercise is generally helpful for persistent non-specific lower back pain, although the most suitable type and dose vary from person to person (Cochrane Review via PubMed, 2021).

While seated, try several slow pelvic tilts, straighten one knee at a time, or gently roll your shoulders. These should feel easy rather than forceful. If the carriage is stable and it is safe to do so, standing for part of the journey can provide a change from sitting. Hold a rail and do not attempt stretches that affect your balance.

Some people use repeated-direction movements, including McKenzie-style exercises, for back or leg symptoms. The direction matters, so an exercise that suits one presentation may aggravate another. If you have already been assessed and given a specific movement, this guide to practical lumbar disc management explains how it may fit around sitting, walking and driving.

What to do after leaving the train

Try not to move straight from the carriage into another long sitting period. An easy walk from the station, even if it is only part of the way home or to work, provides a gentle transition. Staying active within comfortable limits is generally preferred to prolonged bed rest for uncomplicated back pain (Better Health Channel).

If warmth feels soothing, a wrapped heat pack may be used for a short period once you are home. Protect the skin and avoid sleeping on it. Heat can provide temporary comfort for some types of back pain, but it does not identify or correct the cause (Mayo Clinic).

Look at the rest of the commute as well. Alternate the shoulder used for a light bag, or use a backpack with both straps if that feels better. Keep frequently used items easy to reach so you are not repeatedly twisting in a cramped seat. A brief note of which trains, seats and positions aggravate or settle the pain can be more useful than trying to remember several days later.

Know when the commute is not the main issue

Arrange a health assessment if the pain keeps worsening, is not settling, repeatedly travels below the knee, or is accompanied by numbness or weakness. A clinician may assess movement, sensation, strength and reflexes alongside your medical history before discussing suitable care (Mayo Clinic).

Seek urgent medical care for back pain with new loss of bladder or bowel control, numbness around the groin or saddle area, rapidly increasing leg weakness, fever, or pain following a serious accident. These features can indicate a condition requiring prompt medical investigation (healthdirect). If symptoms are severe or you believe there is an emergency, call Triple Zero on 000.

Frequently asked questions

These answers cover common practical questions, but persistent or changing symptoms still need individual assessment.

What is the best sitting posture for lower back pain?

There is no single position that everyone with back pain must maintain. Start with your feet supported and your back resting comfortably, then change position before discomfort builds. General posture advice favours supported, comfortable alignment without excessive tension (Better Health Channel).

Should I stand for the whole train trip?

Not necessarily. Standing continuously can also become uncomfortable, and balance may be difficult on a moving train. If it is safe, alternate between sitting and standing rather than forcing either position.

Should I stretch if pain travels down my leg?

Avoid forcing a strong hamstring or nerve stretch into pain, tingling or numbness. Leg symptoms may be associated with sciatica and should be assessed if they persist, worsen or include weakness (Mayo Clinic).

Does commuting pain mean I have a slipped disc?

No. Symptoms during sitting do not, by themselves, establish that a disc is the cause, and back pain can arise from several factors (Better Health Channel). For a closer look at disc protrusion, sciatica and medical treatment options, read this overview of herniated discs and sciatica.
