Manage Plantar Heel Pain When Walking: Practical Steps

29 October 2026 · Andy Lee, Chiropractor · Foot & heel pain

The first few steps out of bed feel sharp under your heel. It eases after moving around, then returns during a longer walk or after a busy day on your feet. If you are trying to manage plantar heel pain, the immediate aim is usually not to stop walking altogether. It is to find an amount of walking your foot can tolerate while you work on strength, mobility and footwear.

That familiar pattern is often associated with plantar fasciitis, which commonly causes pain near the bottom of the heel and can be worse with the first steps after rest (Mayo Clinic). Similar pain can occasionally come from another problem, so the location, onset and behaviour of your symptoms matter.

How to manage plantar heel pain day to day

Start by looking at what changed before the pain began. A sudden increase in walking, more time on hard floors, different shoes or a return to running may be relevant. Repetitive strain can irritate the plantar fascia, the band of tissue running along the sole that helps support the foot's arch (Mayo Clinic).

For the next week, reduce the part of your routine that clearly stirs it up. That might mean shortening a 45-minute walk, taking a flatter route around Hornsby, or splitting one long outing into two shorter ones. You do not have to chase zero discomfort, but pain that becomes progressively sharper, changes your stride or remains noticeably worse the following morning suggests that the current amount is too much.

Keep a simple note of walking time, shoes worn and how the first steps feel the next morning. This gives you something more useful than guessing from one particularly good or bad day.

Adjust walking without becoming inactive

Complete rest can make it difficult to judge what your heel can handle. Instead, choose a manageable baseline. Walk at a comfortable pace on even ground, then hold that amount steady for several days before increasing it. Current clinical guidance supports combining activity modification with exercises and other targeted care rather than relying on one treatment alone (Journal of Orthopaedic & Sports Physical Therapy, 2023).

During a flare, swap some walking for an activity that places less pressure through the heel, provided it is comfortable and appropriate for you. A stationary bike or swimming may suit some people. Avoid adding hills, speed and distance at the same time. Change one variable, see how the foot responds, then decide on the next step.

  • Shorten the walk before slowing to an unnatural shuffle.
  • Choose a predictable surface while symptoms are irritable.
  • Take a brief seated break rather than pushing until you limp.
  • Build back gradually instead of testing the heel with one large walk.

Check your shoes and short-term support

A comfortable walking shoe should feel secure around the heel, bend near the forefoot rather than folding through the middle, and have enough room for your toes. The most expensive shoe is not automatically the best one. Try shoes later in the day, wear your usual socks and judge them by comfort rather than by a particular brand.

If barefoot walking on tiles or timber floors aggravates the heel, use a supportive indoor shoe for a while. Taping may also provide short-term symptom relief for plantar heel pain, particularly when used alongside exercise rather than as the whole plan (Journal of Orthopaedic & Sports Physical Therapy, 2023).

Prefabricated or custom foot orthoses may help some people as part of broader treatment, but clinical guidelines do not recommend using orthoses as an isolated treatment (Journal of Orthopaedic & Sports Physical Therapy, 2023). If an insert crowds your shoe, causes numbness or shifts pain elsewhere, stop using it and have the fit reviewed.

Stretch, then build the foot's capacity

Stretching the plantar fascia and calf can be useful, especially when first-step pain is prominent. Plantar fascia-specific stretching has been studied as a conservative treatment for chronic plantar heel pain (PubMed). Keep the pressure firm but tolerable rather than forcing through sharp pain.

  • Plantar fascia stretch: Sit, cross the sore foot over the opposite knee and gently pull the toes back until you feel tension through the arch. Hold briefly and repeat, particularly before standing after sleep or prolonged sitting.
  • Calf stretch: Face a wall with the sore leg behind you. Keep the heel down and lean forward without turning the foot out. Try it once with the back knee straight and once slightly bent.
  • Calf raise: Begin with both feet, rising slowly onto the balls of the feet and lowering with control. Use a bench or wall for balance.

Strength work should be scaled to your current capacity. Progressive heel-raise loading has been investigated for plantar fasciitis, with research supporting resistance exercise as one option within conservative care (PubMed). Start on level ground. Adding a step, weight or single-leg work too early may provoke the heel.

When an assessment is worthwhile

Arrange an assessment if the pain is persistent, repeatedly changes how you walk, or is not responding to sensible adjustments. A physical examination commonly looks at the tender area, ankle movement, foot function and other possible sources of pain; imaging is not routinely required for a straightforward presentation but may be used when another condition is suspected (Mayo Clinic).

Seek prompt medical advice after a significant injury, if you cannot bear weight, or if the foot is hot, markedly swollen, red or associated with fever. Numbness, a wound that is not healing, or foot symptoms in someone with diabetes also deserve timely review (healthdirect).

Frequently asked questions

Can I keep walking with plantar heel pain?

Often you can continue with a reduced, tolerable amount rather than stopping completely. Shorten the walk if pain becomes sharper, makes you limp or is clearly worse the next morning. Clinical guidance supports adjusting aggravating activity while using exercise and other appropriate care (Journal of Orthopaedic & Sports Physical Therapy, 2023).

What shoes are best for a painful heel?

Choose shoes that feel comfortable, hold the heel securely and have enough space for your toes. A shoe that reduces symptoms during an ordinary walk is more useful than choosing by brand, price or appearance alone. Footwear changes may be combined with taping, exercise or orthoses where appropriate (Journal of Orthopaedic & Sports Physical Therapy, 2023).

How long does plantar heel pain take to settle?

Recovery time varies with symptom duration, walking demands and how consistently aggravating loads can be adjusted. Many cases improve with conservative care, although progress may take several months rather than a few days (Mayo Clinic). A review is sensible if the pattern is worsening or the diagnosis is unclear.

Should I roll my heel on a ball?

Gentle rolling may feel soothing, but it should not leave the heel bruised or more painful afterwards. Treat it as a comfort measure rather than a substitute for load adjustment and progressive exercise. Manual therapy may be used alongside stretching and strengthening in appropriate cases (Journal of Orthopaedic & Sports Physical Therapy, 2023).
