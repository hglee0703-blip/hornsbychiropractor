Nerve Root Compression on MRI: What It Means

25 November 2026 · Andy Lee, Chiropractor · Lower back pain

Your MRI report arrives and one phrase stands out: nerve root compression on MRI. It sounds serious, especially if pain is already running through your buttock or leg. The phrase means that the scan shows a spinal nerve root being pressed or crowded by nearby tissue, often a disc or narrowing around the nerve (NCBI Bookshelf). It does not, by itself, prove that this finding is causing your pain.

Sciatica refers to symptoms arising from irritation or compression of the sciatic nerve roots, commonly including pain that travels from the lower back or buttock into the leg (healthdirect). Numbness, tingling or weakness can occur as well, but each person’s pattern is different.

What nerve root compression on MRI means

A nerve root is the short section of nerve leaving the spinal canal before it joins other nerves travelling into the leg. In the lower back, a disc protrusion, arthritic change or reduced space around the nerve can produce the appearance of compression (NCBI Bookshelf).

Reports may use terms such as contact, abutment, impingement or compression. These words describe what the radiologist can see, although terminology and grading are not perfectly uniform between reports (Fardon et al., 2014). “Contact” may simply mean that disc material touches a nerve. “Compression” suggests clearer displacement or crowding, but symptoms and examination findings still matter.

The level is useful too. For example, a report may name the L5 or S1 nerve root and whether the finding is on the left or right. Different lumbar nerve roots are associated with different areas of sensation, muscle action and reflexes in the leg (NCBI Bookshelf).

Does the scan explain your sciatica?

Sometimes it fits neatly. Right-sided leg pain, altered sensation and weakness in a pattern matching a compressed right nerve root make the scan more clinically relevant. A finding on the opposite side, or at a level that does not match the symptoms, is less convincing.

MRI changes are also found in people who have no back pain or sciatica. Disc degeneration, bulging and protrusion become more common with age, including among people without symptoms (Brinjikji et al., 2015). That is why an impressive-looking scan can occasionally be incidental, while a person with significant leg pain may have only modest changes reported.

Pain intensity cannot be measured from the image. MRI shows structure, not how much the nerve is inflamed, how sensitive the nervous system has become, or how the symptoms affect sitting, walking and sleep. The scan is one part of the assessment rather than a verdict.

How the MRI is matched with your examination

A clinical assessment usually maps where the pain and altered sensation travel. Strength testing may include ankle movement, lifting the big toe or rising onto the toes, while reflexes and nerve-tension tests can provide further information about the affected root (NCBI Bookshelf).

The history matters just as much. I would want to know when the leg symptoms began, whether coughing or sitting aggravates them, how far the pain travels, and whether strength is changing. Other conditions can resemble sciatica, so the hip, circulation and peripheral nerves may also need consideration.

MRI is generally most useful when symptoms are persistent, severe, worsening, associated with neurological loss, or likely to change treatment decisions. Routine early imaging is not recommended for uncomplicated lower back pain without concerning features because it often does not improve care (American College of Radiology, 2016).

What treatment may involve

Treatment is guided by your symptoms and function, not the word “compression” alone. Many cases are initially managed without surgery, using suitable activity, exercise and symptom relief while monitoring nerve function (healthdirect). Prolonged bed rest is generally discouraged; activity may need to be reduced or modified for a while rather than stopped completely.

Hands-on treatment may be considered for accompanying lower back pain, but it should be individualised and should not be described as pushing a disc back into place. A sensible plan may include graded movement, advice about lifting and sitting, and exercises selected around pain and neurological findings. Medication should be discussed with a GP or pharmacist because suitability depends on your health, other medicines and possible adverse effects. If disabling leg pain persists, a medical specialist may discuss an epidural injection or surgery; epidural corticosteroid injections tend to offer only small, short-term improvements for some people with lumbosacral radicular pain (Cochrane review, 2020). The choice depends on symptom severity, neurological loss, duration and personal circumstances. When the finding needs urgent attention

Seek urgent medical assessment if sciatica is accompanied by new difficulty passing or controlling urine, loss of bowel control, numbness around the genitals or anus, or marked weakness affecting both legs. These can be features of cauda equina syndrome, a rare condition requiring emergency assessment (NCBI Bookshelf).

Rapidly worsening weakness in one leg also deserves prompt review. Significant trauma, fever, unexplained weight loss, a history of cancer or severe pain that is not settling may change how urgently further investigation is needed (American College of Radiology, 2016). If you are uncertain about sudden or severe symptoms in Australia, contact your GP, an emergency department or healthdirect on 1800 022 222.

Frequently asked questions

Does nerve root compression always cause sciatica?

No. MRI can show disc and nerve-root changes in people without symptoms, so compression must be compared with the side, location and pattern of your leg symptoms (Brinjikji et al., 2015). A physical examination helps determine whether the finding is likely to be relevant.

Can nerve root compression improve without surgery?

Many cases of sciatica are managed without surgery, particularly when strength is stable and emergency warning signs are absent (healthdirect). Recovery time varies, and ongoing or worsening neurological loss should be medically reviewed.

Is a compressed nerve the same as a trapped nerve?

“Trapped nerve” is an informal phrase rather than a precise MRI diagnosis. It is often used to describe nerve irritation or compression, but the report’s level, side and wording provide more useful detail.

Should I stop exercising after this MRI result?

Usually not, unless a clinician has identified a reason to restrict activity. Exercise may need to be scaled back or altered so that leg symptoms and weakness can be monitored, while prolonged bed rest is generally discouraged for sciatica (healthdirect).
