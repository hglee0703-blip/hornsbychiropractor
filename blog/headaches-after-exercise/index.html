Headaches After Exercise: Causes and When to Get Help

19 October 2026 · Andy Lee, Chiropractor · Headaches

You finish a hard run, a set of squats or a social game of tennis, then notice a pounding pain across your head. Headaches after exercise are sometimes linked with exertion, heat or dehydration, but a new or sudden headache can also need prompt medical assessment (Mayo Clinic).

The way the pain began matters. So do its intensity, how long it lasts and whether anything else feels wrong. A headache that gradually appears during a familiar workout is a different situation from severe pain that reaches full intensity within seconds.

What headaches after exercise can mean

A primary exercise headache occurs during or after strenuous physical activity without another underlying disorder being found. It may feel throbbing, can affect both sides of the head and is often associated with activities such as running, rowing, weightlifting or other sustained exertion (Mayo Clinic).

Doctors use the word primary only after considering secondary causes. Exercise-related head pain can occasionally be associated with bleeding around the brain, blood vessel abnormalities or another medical problem, which is why an unfamiliar pattern should not simply be labelled an exertion headache at home (Mayo Clinic).

Dehydration can also cause headache, particularly if exercise has involved heavy sweating or hot weather. Thirst, a dry mouth, dark urine, dizziness and passing less urine can accompany dehydration (healthdirect). Around Sydney, this is worth keeping in mind during humid summer sessions and outdoor sport.

Clues worth noticing

Try to remember exactly what you were doing when the pain started. Heavy lifting and activities involving straining may provoke an exercise headache, while heat and high altitude can make this type of headache more likely (Mayo Clinic). That does not confirm the cause, but it gives your clinician useful context.

Before an appointment, note:

  • whether the pain started during exercise or afterwards
  • whether it built gradually or arrived suddenly
  • where it was felt and whether it was pounding, sharp or pressure-like
  • how long it lasted and whether stopping activity changed it
  • the temperature, your fluid intake and how hard you were working
  • any nausea, vomiting, visual disturbance, neck stiffness, weakness or balance trouble.

Head pain can sometimes be referred from structures in the upper neck. Cervicogenic headache is usually associated with a neck disorder and may be accompanied by restricted neck movement or pain triggered by neck position (NCBI Bookshelf). Pain around the upper neck or back of the head may also be discussed in this guide to headache at the base of the skull. Still, exercise timing alone does not establish that the neck is responsible.

When the headache needs urgent help

Stop exercising and seek urgent medical care if the headache is abrupt, unusually severe or unlike anything you have experienced before. Mayo Clinic advises contacting a doctor promptly for a headache during or after exercise, particularly if it begins suddenly or is your first headache of this kind (Mayo Clinic).

Call Triple Zero (000) if the situation appears life-threatening, rather than attempting to drive yourself (healthdirect). Warning signs include:

  • a sudden, extremely painful or “worst-ever” headache (healthdirect)
  • weakness or numbness on one side, facial drooping, difficulty speaking, confusion, loss of balance or sudden visual trouble, which can be signs of stroke (healthdirect)
  • fainting, a seizure or difficulty staying awake (healthdirect)
  • fever with a stiff neck, marked drowsiness or a new rash, which can occur with meningitis (healthdirect)
  • a worsening headache after a significant knock to the head (healthdirect).

A severe or persistent headache during pregnancy also deserves prompt assessment because headache can be a symptom of pre-eclampsia, especially when accompanied by visual changes, sudden swelling or pain below the ribs (Pregnancy, Birth and Baby).

What to do before your next workout

If the headache is mild and there are no warning signs, stop the session rather than pushing through it. Move somewhere cool, rest and replace fluids gradually if you may be dehydrated (healthdirect). If symptoms persist, recur or feel unusual, arrange a medical review before returning to hard exercise.

A gradual warm-up may help prevent some primary exercise headaches (Mayo Clinic). It is also sensible to build training load progressively instead of jumping straight into maximal sprints or heavy lifts after time away. The same measured approach is covered in this article on returning to exercise safely.

Avoid repeatedly using pain medicine simply to get through workouts without discussing the pattern with a health professional. Frequent use of acute headache medicines can itself contribute to medication-overuse headache (Mayo Clinic).

Who should assess it?

Your GP is a sensible starting point for a first, severe, recurring or changing exercise headache. Assessment may include questions about the onset and associated symptoms, a physical and neurological examination, and sometimes imaging when the pain is sudden or other concerning features are present (Mayo Clinic).

If the headache seems to occur alongside neck pain, restricted movement or sustained training posture, a musculoskeletal assessment may form one part of the process. In clinic, my priority is to screen for features that require medical referral before considering whether the neck or surrounding joints and muscles may be relevant. Bring a short record of two or three episodes if you have one. Timing, exercise intensity, hydration, associated symptoms and recovery are often more useful than trying to remember everything during the consultation.

Frequently asked questions

Are headaches after exercise normal?

They can occur as a primary exercise headache, but “common” does not mean every episode should be ignored. Arrange an assessment if this is your first exercise-related headache, it keeps returning, changes pattern or starts suddenly (Mayo Clinic).

Can dehydration cause a headache after training?

Yes. Headache can occur with dehydration, often alongside thirst, dry mouth, dizziness, dark urine or reduced urination (healthdirect). Severe dehydration, confusion or fainting needs urgent medical care.

Could the pain be coming from my neck?

Possibly, particularly if there is neck pain, limited movement or pain provoked by neck position. Cervicogenic headache refers pain from a neck disorder into the head, but an examination is needed because symptoms can overlap with other headache types (NCBI Bookshelf).

When can I exercise again?

There is no single waiting period that suits every headache. Do not return to hard training while symptoms remain or before a sudden, severe, first-time or otherwise concerning headache has been assessed. Once serious causes have been excluded, a clinician can help you plan a gradual return based on the likely trigger and your usual activity.
