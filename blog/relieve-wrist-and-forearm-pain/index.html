Relieve Wrist and Forearm Pain From Mouse Use

03 October 2026 · Andy Lee, Chiropractor · Arm, wrist & hand

You are halfway through a morning at the computer when the wrist holding your mouse starts to ache. By lunchtime, the discomfort may have spread along the forearm or towards the elbow. To relieve wrist and forearm pain, start by changing how much work the hand is doing and where the mouse sits, rather than trying to push through until the end of the day.

Mouse-related pain is not one single condition. Tendons, muscles, joints and nerves can all produce symptoms in this area, and the location of pain alone does not confirm which tissue is involved (healthdirect).

How to relieve wrist and forearm pain at work

First, reduce the task that is stirring things up. That might mean using keyboard shortcuts, alternating between mouse and keyboard work, or moving some calls away from the screen. Frequent brief pauses are usually more practical than waiting until pain forces one long break. Healthdirect recommends changing or reducing the activity linked with repetitive strain symptoms while the area settles (healthdirect).

Try not to grip the mouse. Let the hand rest over it and use the lightest click pressure that works. If scrolling is the main irritant, page-up and page-down keys can temporarily reduce repeated finger movement. Switching the mouse to the other hand may help for short periods, though it can create a new problem if you suddenly use the unfamiliar side for a full working day.

Complete rest is rarely a practical plan for computer workers. Aim for a tolerable level of activity instead. If a change clearly causes sharper pain, spreading tingling or increasing symptoms that linger afterwards, scale it back rather than treating the reaction as something to work through.

Bring the mouse closer before buying new equipment

A mouse positioned too far forward or out to the side makes the shoulder and forearm work harder to reach it. Place it close to the keyboard, with the upper arm relaxed beside your body. Keep the wrist roughly in line with the forearm rather than bent upwards or sideways. These points are consistent with Mayo Clinic office ergonomics guidance (Mayo Clinic).

Check the chair and desk together. Your forearm should be supported without the desk edge pressing firmly into it, and the mouse should move freely without requiring repeated lifting. A keyboard tray that is too narrow can push the mouse further away. On a laptop, a separate keyboard and mouse usually allow more flexible positioning than relying on the trackpad while the screen is raised.

An ergonomic mouse, trackball or pen-style device is not automatically better. It may redistribute the load, which can be useful, but comfort depends on hand size, task and technique. If possible, trial a device before buying it. Change one part of the setup at a time so you can tell whether it actually helps.

Use gentle movement without repeatedly testing the pain

During a pause, open and close the hand slowly a few times. Turn the palm upwards and downwards with the elbow tucked near your side, then gently bend and straighten the elbow. The aim is comfortable movement, not a strong stretch.

You can also try a mild forearm stretch. With the elbow almost straight, use the other hand to guide the wrist down until you feel a light pull through the top of the forearm. For the opposite side, turn the palm upwards and gently extend the wrist. Hold briefly and stop if the movement produces tingling, numbness or sharp pain.

Strength work may be appropriate once ordinary mouse use is more settled, but loading an irritable wrist with repeated curls or grip exercises can aggravate it. Exercise selection should reflect the likely source of symptoms and your current capacity. A clinician can help with this if you are unsure, especially where weakness or nerve symptoms are present.

Pay attention to where the symptoms travel

An ache through the forearm that builds with clicking or gripping may fit an overuse pattern, but some symptom combinations deserve closer attention. Numbness or tingling in the thumb, index and middle fingers, symptoms that wake you at night, or reduced hand strength can occur with carpal tunnel syndrome (Mayo Clinic).

Pain and swelling on the thumb side of the wrist, particularly with gripping or moving the thumb, may occur with de Quervain tenosynovitis (Mayo Clinic). Pain near the outer elbow can also be related to the forearm tendons used during gripping and wrist movement (Better Health Channel).

Symptoms can overlap, so avoid diagnosing the problem from an online diagram alone. Also consider the rest of your workstation. If your shoulder creeps upwards or your head sits forward while mousing, the whole arm may stay tense. The same desk adjustments discussed in this guide to neck pain from sitting at a computer can be useful here, as can the pacing ideas in the article on relieving posture-related tension.

When the wrist or forearm should be assessed

Arrange an assessment if symptoms are persisting, recurring whenever you return to normal work, disturbing sleep or affecting grip. Seek prompt medical care after a significant injury, or if there is marked swelling, deformity, inability to move the hand, loss of feeling, or a hand that becomes unusually pale, blue or cold.

An assessment may include the neck, shoulder, elbow, wrist and hand rather than focusing only on the sore spot. Depending on the findings, management might involve modifying work, graded exercise, hands-on care or referral to a GP, physiotherapist or hand specialist. Frequently asked questions

Should I wear a wrist brace while using a mouse?

A brace may help some people keep the wrist from bending, but it should not make gripping the mouse harder or press into the forearm. Night splinting is sometimes used for carpal tunnel syndrome, although a brace is not the right answer for every cause of wrist pain (healthdirect). If symptoms increase in the brace, stop using it and seek advice.

Is a trackball better than a normal mouse?

Not for everyone. A trackball reduces some arm movement but asks the fingers or thumb to work differently, so the result depends on where your symptoms are and how you use it. Trial it gradually rather than changing devices during a busy full day.

How long should mouse-related wrist pain take to settle?

There is no reliable timetable because a mild workload flare is different from nerve compression, tendon pain or an injury. Improvement should be judged by whether everyday tasks are becoming easier and symptoms are less reactive. Arrange an assessment if pain is worsening, keeps returning, affects sleep or is accompanied by numbness or weakness.
