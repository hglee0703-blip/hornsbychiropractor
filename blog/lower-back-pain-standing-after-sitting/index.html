Lower Back Pain When Standing After Sitting

02 September 2026 · Lower back pain

You get out of the car, rise from the sofa or push back from your desk, and your lower back catches. For a few seconds you may feel bent forward, stiff or reluctant to take the first step. Sometimes it eases once you move. At other times, the ache hangs around.

This pattern can come from several structures and does not automatically mean that something has slipped out of place. Lower back pain is commonly described as non-specific because the exact tissue responsible cannot always be identified from symptoms or imaging alone (The Lancet, 2018).

What changes while you are sitting?

Sitting holds your hips and lower back in a fairly fixed position. If you stay there for a while, the first movement afterwards may feel stiff or painful, particularly when the area is already sensitive. That does not necessarily mean the sitting position has caused fresh damage. Back pain can be influenced by physical loading, sleep, stress, general health and previous episodes, rather than one posture in isolation (The Lancet, 2018).

The transition itself also asks your body to do quite a bit. Your trunk has to move over your feet, your hips need to straighten and the muscles around your abdomen, pelvis and spine need to coordinate the effort. A low chair, deep lounge or soft car seat can make that task harder because you start from a more folded position.

Pay attention to what happens next. Pain that eases after several comfortable steps presents a different picture from pain that travels down the leg, steadily worsens or remains severe regardless of position.

Common reasons the movement may hurt

A temporary flare of non-specific mechanical back pain is one possibility. Muscles, ligaments, spinal joints and discs can all contribute to lower back symptoms, but examination findings do not always identify one structure with certainty (The Lancet, 2018). Recent lifting, an abrupt increase in exercise, a long drive or spending much more time seated than usual may be useful clues.

A lumbar disc may sometimes be involved, especially if sitting aggravates the pain and there is pain, tingling, numbness or weakness extending into a leg. Sciatica refers to symptoms caused by irritation or compression of the sciatic nerve roots, commonly associated with a herniated disc or age-related narrowing around the nerve (Mayo Clinic). Leg symptoms do not confirm a disc problem by themselves, so assessment matters. Hip conditions can also be felt around the buttock, groin or lower back, while inflammatory disorders may produce prolonged morning stiffness and pain that improves with activity rather than rest (Mayo Clinic). These are not conclusions to draw from one symptom, but they are reasons to look beyond the back if the pattern persists.

What to try when you get up

Before standing, bring your feet back underneath you and move towards the front of the chair. Lean forward from the hips, use the armrests if needed, then stand without twisting. Once upright, pause briefly and take a few easy steps rather than forcing yourself immediately into a rigid, fully arched posture.

During the day, change position regularly instead of searching for one perfect sitting posture. A short walk, standing phone call or brief movement break may be more practical than trying to hold yourself still. Staying active within tolerable limits is generally recommended for uncomplicated lower back pain, while prolonged bed rest can delay recovery (healthdirect). Exercise is also supported for managing ongoing non-specific lower back pain, although the most suitable type and starting level vary between people (Cochrane).

Heat may feel soothing for some people, and non-prescription pain medicine may occasionally be appropriate. Medicines have risks and are not suitable for everyone, particularly with pregnancy, kidney disease, stomach ulcers, cardiovascular conditions or interactions with other medicines, so check with a pharmacist or GP rather than relying on general advice (healthdirect). If leg pain is a major feature, this article on disc protrusion, sciatica, injections and surgery explains the broader treatment options and their limitations.

When an assessment makes sense

Arrange an assessment if the pain is not settling, repeatedly limits work or sleep, follows a significant injury, or is accompanied by persistent leg symptoms. A clinician may ask about the pain pattern, general health and recent activity, then examine spinal and hip movement, strength, sensation and reflexes. Imaging is usually reserved for situations where the result is likely to change management or a serious cause is suspected, because scan findings do not always explain a person’s pain (The Lancet, 2018).

Seek urgent medical care for new loss of bladder or bowel control, numbness around the genitals or anus, marked or worsening leg weakness, back pain after major trauma, or pain accompanied by fever or unexplained weight loss. These features can indicate a condition requiring prompt investigation (Mayo Clinic). In Australia, call triple zero if symptoms are severe or you cannot safely get medical help another way.

Frequently asked questions

Does pain on standing mean I have a slipped disc?

No. This symptom can occur with several forms of lower back pain, and the painful structure often cannot be identified from the movement alone (The Lancet, 2018). A disc or nerve problem becomes more relevant if you also have persistent pain, numbness, tingling or weakness travelling into a leg.

Should I stretch before getting out of the chair?

You do not need an elaborate routine. A few gentle pelvic or hip movements may feel comfortable, but do not force a stretch into sharp pain or increasing leg symptoms. Regular, tolerable activity is generally preferred to prolonged rest for uncomplicated back pain (healthdirect).

Is a firm chair better for lower back pain?

A chair that lets you place your feet on the floor and stand without a large effort may be easier than a deep, soft seat. Comfort also varies, so change positions rather than trying to maintain one supposedly ideal posture for hours.

Can chiropractic care help this type of pain?

Spinal manipulation is one non-drug option included in clinical guidance for some people with acute or chronic non-specific lower back pain, but expected benefits are generally modest and it is not suitable for every presentation (American College of Physicians guideline, 2017). A proper assessment should come before treatment, particularly if there are leg symptoms, neurological changes, recent trauma or other warning signs.
