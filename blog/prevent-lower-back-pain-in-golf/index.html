Prevent Lower Back Pain in Golf: Practical Tips

06 October 2026 · Andy Lee, Chiropractor · Lower back pain

Your tee time is getting close, so you step out of the car, pull the driver from the bag and take a few quick swings. That rushed start is common, particularly for an early weekend round. If you want to prevent lower back pain in golf, the preparation before your first full swing deserves as much thought as your technique.

A golf swing asks the trunk and hips to rotate quickly while the feet remain planted. Repeating that movement, especially when tired or stiff, can place considerable load through the lower back. Reviews of golf-related back pain identify swing mechanics, physical conditioning and practice volume as relevant factors (NCBI, 2015). You do not need a perfect body or textbook swing, but you do need enough capacity for the way you play.

Prevent lower back pain in golf before teeing off

Give yourself time to move before reaching for the driver. A useful warm-up should gradually raise your effort and rehearse the movements you are about to use. Research across several sports suggests that structured warm-ups can help reduce some sporting injuries, although the most suitable routine varies with the activity and the person (PubMed, 2006).

Start with a brisk walk around the practice area. Follow it with comfortable trunk turns, hip hinges and shallow bodyweight squats. Nothing should be forced into pain.

  • Make slow practice swings with a short club.
  • Move on to half swings before hitting full shots.
  • Hit several easy wedges before longer clubs.
  • Allow both sides of your body to move rather than holding your hips rigid.

A few hard driver swings are not much of a warm-up. Golf-specific reviews recommend progressive movement and attention to flexibility and conditioning rather than beginning at full speed (NCBI, 2015). On a cool Sydney morning, you may simply need a little longer to feel ready.

Build strength for the swing you have

The aim of strength work is not to make your back rigid. It is to help your trunk, hips and legs share the work across repeated swings. Exercise therapy can improve pain and function for many people with chronic non-specific lower back pain, though no single exercise approach suits everybody (Cochrane via PubMed, 2021).

  • Practise a controlled hip hinge without rounding or straining.
  • Use bridges or sit-to-stands to train the hips and legs.
  • Try light carries while keeping a comfortable, upright posture.
  • Use gentle resistance exercises that ask the trunk to control rotation.

Build resistance gradually and leave recovery time between harder sessions. Australian physical activity guidance recommends muscle-strengthening activity on at least two days each week for adults (Australian Government Department of Health). If gym training tends to stir your symptoms, these suggestions for managing lower back pain after lifting weights may also be useful.

Make your swing and practice load more manageable

Trying to create more distance by forcing a bigger turn can be costly if your hips or upper back do not move comfortably. The lower back may then take more of the rotation. Golf biomechanics research has linked aspects of swing technique and repeated loading with lower back symptoms, although pain usually cannot be blamed on one movement alone (NCBI, 2015).

A golf professional can help you find a repeatable swing that suits your current mobility and playing goals. That may be more useful than copying a tour player’s position from a video. If a particular change repeatedly produces pain, stop drilling it and have the movement reviewed.

Practice volume matters too. Hitting a large basket quickly is very different from playing a round with pauses between shots. Change clubs, take short walking breaks and finish before fatigue makes every swing feel like an effort. Pick balls up by bending through your hips and knees, or use the club for light support, rather than repeatedly twisting and reaching from the waist.

Look after your back across the full round

A round involves far more than swinging. Carrying or pulling equipment, bending to mark the ball and sitting in a cart all add to the day’s workload. Fatigue and carrying a heavy bag have been discussed as potentially modifiable contributors to golf-related lower back pain (NCBI, 2015).

  • Remove items from your bag that you rarely use.
  • Use both straps if carrying, adjusted so the bag sits securely.
  • Consider a trolley if carrying aggravates your back.
  • Keep moving gently while waiting rather than staying bent or seated.

Do not save every ounce of effort for the final few holes. Eat, drink and pace yourself according to the weather and course. If your technique becomes rushed as you tire, shorten the swing or choose a club that does not require an all-out effort. A controlled shot is usually a more sensible choice than trying to overpower a stiff back.

What to do if your back starts hurting

A mild ache is a prompt to adjust, not a challenge to swing harder. Pause, walk for a few minutes and try an easy practice movement. If the pain sharpens, changes your coordination or worsens with each swing, stopping the round is the safer option.

For uncomplicated lower back pain, prolonged bed rest is generally discouraged and a gradual return to ordinary activity is usually recommended (healthdirect). That does not mean playing through significant pain. Walking, gentle movement and a staged return may be more appropriate, and this guide to returning to exercise after lower back pain explains how to build activity back up.

Seek prompt medical assessment if back pain follows major trauma or comes with fever, new bladder or bowel changes, numbness around the groin, or marked weakness or numbness in the legs, as these can be warning signs requiring urgent care (healthdirect). For persistent or recurring pain, an individual assessment can help separate a manageable training issue from something that needs medical investigation. Frequently asked questions

Should I wear a back brace while playing golf?

A brace may feel supportive for some people, but research has not established lumbar supports as a reliable way to prevent lower back pain (Cochrane via PubMed, 2008). It should not replace conditioning, load management or an assessment when symptoms persist.

Can I keep playing with mild lower back pain?

It depends on how the pain behaves. Gentle activity is generally preferable to prolonged rest for uncomplicated back pain, but stop if symptoms are worsening, affecting your swing or travelling into the leg (healthdirect). Seek advice if you are unsure.

Which exercises are best for a golfer’s lower back?

There is no single best exercise for every golfer. A useful plan usually develops leg and hip strength, trunk control and tolerance for rotation, with the difficulty matched to the person. Exercise therapy can help chronic non-specific lower back pain, but programmes should be adjusted for symptoms and ability (Cochrane via PubMed, 2021).

Is stretching enough before a round?

I would not rely on a few held stretches as your entire preparation. A more practical warm-up combines comfortable mobility, a little walking and golf swings that progress from easy to full effort. Evidence supports structured warm-ups rather than an abrupt start to sport (PubMed, 2006).
