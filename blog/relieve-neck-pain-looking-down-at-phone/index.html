Neck pain from looking down at your phone

11 September 2026 · Neck & posture

You are standing on the train, replying to messages with your phone near your waist. Twenty minutes later, the base of your neck feels tight and your shoulders have crept upwards. The same thing can happen on the sofa, at the kitchen table or while scrolling in bed.

Neck discomfort linked with phone use often feels like an ache or stiffness across the neck and upper shoulders. Headaches and pain spreading towards an arm can also occur with some neck conditions, although these symptoms should not automatically be blamed on your phone (Mayo Clinic).

Why looking down can make your neck sore

Your head is not suddenly becoming heavier, but bending it forward changes the mechanical demand on the neck. A frequently cited biomechanical model found that the calculated force on the cervical spine increased as forward head angle increased, though a model like this cannot tell us whether an individual person has injured their neck (Surgical Technology International via PubMed, 2014).

Position is only part of the picture. Time matters too. Even a fairly comfortable posture may become unpleasant if you hold it without moving, particularly while gripping the phone or lifting your shoulders. Neck pain can have several causes, including muscle strain, joint changes, injury and nerve irritation, so posture should not be treated as the only possible explanation (Mayo Clinic).

Change the position before buying equipment

Start by bringing the phone closer to eye level rather than dropping your chin all the way towards your chest. Rest your elbows on a desk, armrest, cushion or your opposite hand if that makes the position easier. You do not need to hold the screen perfectly upright. The aim is simply to reduce how far and how long you bend forward.

Try changing hands and using both thumbs rather than repeatedly holding the phone on one side. For longer emails, forms or reading sessions, move to a laptop or desktop if one is available. Voice-to-text can also reduce the amount of time spent staring down, although it is not practical in every setting.

There is no single ideal posture that must be maintained all day. A more useful target is a comfortable position that you change regularly. Put the phone down between tasks, look across the room, or stand while making a call. These small resets are often easier to maintain than a rigid posture rule.

Gentle movement and exercise

Exercise is commonly included in the management of mechanical neck pain, with evidence suggesting that some strengthening and endurance exercises can help, although the most suitable type and dose vary between people (Cochrane, 2015). The movements below are general options for a mildly stiff neck, not a test of how far you can push.

  • Small chin glide: Sit tall and slide your chin gently backwards, as if making a slight double chin. Keep your eyes level. Hold briefly, relax, and repeat a few comfortable times.
  • Easy neck turns: Slowly turn your head to one side, return to the middle, then turn the other way. Stay within a range that feels smooth rather than forcing the final few degrees.
  • Shoulder-blade movement: Let your arms rest by your sides and gently draw your shoulder blades back and down. Avoid squeezing hard or lifting your chest dramatically.
  • Upper-back extension: Sit against a supportive chair and gently lean your upper back over the backrest while keeping the movement comfortable.

A mild stretching sensation can be acceptable, but sharp pain, increasing arm pain, pins and needles, numbness or weakness are reasons to stop. Symptoms affecting an arm or hand can occur when a nerve is irritated and warrant professional assessment if they persist or worsen (Mayo Clinic).

What else may help, and when to seek care

Heat can feel soothing for a stiff neck. Mayo Clinic includes short applications of heat or cold among common self-care options for minor neck pain (Mayo Clinic). Protect your skin, avoid very hot packs, and do not fall asleep with a heat pack in place.

Keep doing normal daily activities where they are tolerable rather than spending long periods completely still. At night, choose a pillow height that feels supportive and does not leave your head markedly tilted up or down. If you use your phone in bed, prop your arms and screen up rather than curling tightly over it.

Arrange an assessment if the pain is getting worse, keeps returning, follows an injury, or is accompanied by persistent pain, tingling, numbness or weakness in an arm. Urgent medical attention is appropriate after major trauma or if neck pain occurs with marked weakness, trouble walking, loss of bladder or bowel control, fever, or a severe headache (healthdirect Australia). Assessment may involve checking neck movement, strength, sensation and reflexes; imaging is not automatically required for every sore neck (Mayo Clinic).

This article provides general information only. It is not a diagnosis or individual treatment advice. Speak with a qualified health professional about symptoms that concern you or before starting exercises if you have an injury or medical condition.

Frequently asked questions

These are brief answers to common practical questions. Your own symptoms and health history may change what is appropriate.

Is text neck a permanent injury?

Looking down at a phone does not, by itself, establish that permanent damage has occurred. Neck pain has several possible causes, and an examination may be needed if symptoms persist, worsen or spread into an arm (Mayo Clinic).

How often should I take a break from my phone?

There is no universal break interval that suits everyone. Change position before discomfort builds, and use natural pauses such as finishing a message, video or page as reminders to lower your arms, move your neck and look away from the screen.

Should I crack my neck when it feels stiff?

A small click during ordinary movement is not necessarily a sign that something is wrong, but repeatedly forcing your neck to crack is not a reliable way to address the cause of pain. If stiffness keeps returning, have it assessed rather than pushing into painful or forceful movements.

When should phone-related neck pain be checked?

Seek professional advice if it is worsening, follows an injury, does not settle, or comes with arm pain, pins and needles, numbness or weakness. Major trauma, trouble walking, fever, loss of bladder or bowel control, marked weakness or a severe headache require prompt medical attention (healthdirect Australia).
