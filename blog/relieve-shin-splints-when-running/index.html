Relieve Shin Splints When Running: A Safe Return

07 November 2026 · Andy Lee, Chiropractor · Running injuries

You have started running again, perhaps after winter, an injury or a busy stretch at work. The first few runs feel fine, then an ache develops along the inside of the shin. To relieve shin splints when running, the useful starting point is usually to reduce the load irritating the area, rather than trying to stretch or massage through every run.

“Shin splints” commonly refers to medial tibial stress syndrome, which causes exercise-related pain along the inner border of the shin bone. Similar pain can also come from a bone stress injury, muscle problem or, less commonly, exertional compartment syndrome, so the exact pattern matters (NCBI Bookshelf).

Work out what the shin pain may be

Typical shin splint pain is spread over a broader area along the inner shin and is linked to running or other repetitive loading. Tenderness at one very specific point, pain at rest or pain that is getting worse despite reducing activity deserves more caution because stress fractures can produce increasingly localised pain and may eventually hurt during ordinary walking (Mayo Clinic).

Also pay attention to swelling, numbness, weakness or a tight bursting sensation that predictably appears with exercise. Those features are not a reason to keep testing the leg on another run; they warrant a proper assessment (NCBI Bookshelf).

How to relieve shin splints when running

For now, shorten or pause the runs that reproduce the pain. Ice wrapped in a towel may help with short-term soreness, while comfortable low-impact exercise such as swimming or cycling can be used if it does not aggravate the shin (Mayo Clinic).

Avoid repeatedly pressing hard into the tender edge of the bone. Gentle calf movement is reasonable, but aggressive stretching is not a substitute for changing the training load that brought the symptoms on. Research into conservative treatment has not identified one reliably superior treatment, which is why management is usually built around individual load adjustment and progressive rehabilitation rather than a quick fix (PubMed).

Adjust your training load without stopping everything

Look back over the fortnight before the pain started. Common triggers include a sudden increase in running frequency, distance, speed or hills, as well as returning after a period with little impact exercise (Mayo Clinic). A new route can matter too. The slopes and longer descents around parts of Hornsby may load the legs differently from a flat treadmill or oval.

Reduce one or two variables rather than changing everything at once. That might mean running less often, choosing flatter ground and leaving faster sessions out temporarily. If walking, cycling or pool exercise is comfortable, it can give you something active to do while the shin settles (Mayo Clinic).

Build a gradual return to running

Start once normal walking and everyday stairs are comfortable, and the shin is not becoming more tender from day to day. A run-walk session on level ground is often easier to control than heading out for an uninterrupted distance. For example, alternate short, relaxed running intervals with walking, then wait to see how the shin feels later that day and the next morning.

If symptoms stay mild and settle rather than building, increase only one part of training at a time. Add a little running duration before bringing back hills, intervals or consecutive running days. Pain that becomes sharper, changes your stride or remains worse the following day is a sign to step back and reassess rather than push the schedule forward (NCBI Bookshelf).

If an ankle injury preceded the problem, the progression in returning to running after an ankle sprain may also be useful. Runners noticing back symptoms as their distance increases can read about lower back pain during running.

Use strength, footwear and terrain sensibly

Calf raises with a straight knee and a slightly bent knee are practical starting exercises, provided they do not increase the shin pain. Foot and ankle control, balance and hip strength may also be considered according to what an examination finds, but there is no single exercise routine that suits every case (PubMed).

Check that your running shoes are comfortable, fit properly and are not obviously worn through or uneven. Supportive footwear and, in selected cases, shoe inserts may form part of care, although they should not be presented as a guaranteed cure for shin pain (Mayo Clinic). Softer or flatter routes may feel better initially, but the longer-term aim is to rebuild capacity for the surfaces you actually want to run on.

When an assessment is worthwhile

Arrange an assessment if the pain is strongly localised, affects walking, occurs at rest or at night, keeps returning, or is not settling after you reduce the provoking activity. Severe pain after an injury, marked swelling, numbness or difficulty bearing weight needs more prompt medical attention (healthdirect).

During an appointment, I would look at the exact pain location, recent training changes, ankle and foot movement, calf capacity and how you load the leg. Imaging is not routinely needed for a straightforward presentation, but a clinician may arrange an X-ray, MRI or other investigation when a stress fracture or another condition is suspected (Mayo Clinic). Frequently asked questions

These are common practical questions during the first few weeks of returning to running.

Can I keep running with shin splints?

That depends on the pain pattern and whether a bone stress injury has been ruled out. If discomfort is mild, spread along the shin and settles after reducing the session, modified running may be reasonable; sharp, worsening or highly localised pain should be assessed before you continue (NCBI Bookshelf).

How long should I rest from running?

There is no fixed rest period that suits every sore shin. Base the return on comfortable daily activity, improving tenderness and your response to a small test session rather than choosing an arbitrary date (Mayo Clinic).

Should I stretch my calves?

Gentle calf stretching may feel comfortable, but forcing a stretch into pain is unlikely to address an excessive running load. Calf strengthening and a graded return may also be included, although evidence does not support one universal treatment plan (PubMed).

How can I tell shin splints from a stress fracture?

Shin splint tenderness is usually spread over a longer section of the inner shin, while stress fracture pain is more likely to be focused at one spot and may progress to pain with walking or at rest. Symptoms overlap, so examination and sometimes imaging are needed when a stress fracture is suspected (Mayo Clinic).
