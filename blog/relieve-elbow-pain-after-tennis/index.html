Relieve Elbow Pain After Tennis: A Practical Guide

22 October 2026 · Andy Lee, Chiropractor · Elbow & forearm pain

You finish a match, put the racquet away and notice an ache around the outside or inside of your elbow. By the next morning, gripping a mug or turning a door handle may feel surprisingly uncomfortable. If you are trying to relieve elbow pain after tennis, the first few days should be about reducing the irritation without leaving the arm completely idle.

Tennis is an obvious trigger, but the racquet is only part of the picture. A sudden increase in playing time, repeated late contact, a tight grip or extra activity away from the court can all add to the load on the forearm. Most tennis-related elbow pain can be managed without surgery, although recovery may take time and the correct approach depends on what has actually been irritated (Mayo Clinic).

Work out which part of the elbow is sore

Pain on the bony outside of the elbow is commonly associated with lateral elbow tendinopathy, often called tennis elbow. It may hurt during gripping, lifting with the palm facing down or using the backhand. Pain on the inside can involve the forearm tendons on the other side and is often labelled golfer’s elbow, even when tennis caused it (Mayo Clinic; Mayo Clinic).

Not every sore elbow is a tendon problem. Pain can also come from a joint injury, nerve irritation or referred pain from elsewhere in the arm. Tingling into the hand, marked weakness, locking, a significant loss of movement or pain following a fall deserves a proper assessment rather than an assumed diagnosis.

How to relieve elbow pain after tennis

Pause the strokes and exercises that clearly aggravate the pain for a few days. That does not necessarily mean complete rest. Comfortable walking, lower-body training and ordinary arm movements can usually continue, provided they do not provoke the elbow. Ice wrapped in a towel may help with short-term discomfort after activity, but it should not be placed directly on the skin (Mayo Clinic).

Look at the less obvious loading as well. Carrying shopping with the sore arm, using tools, lifting a laptop bag and long periods at a mouse can keep the area grumpy. If computer work is contributing, these suggestions for wrist and forearm pain from mouse use may help you adjust the rest of your week.

Over-the-counter pain relief is not suitable for everyone. Anti-inflammatory medicines can interact with other medicines and may be unsafe with some stomach, kidney, heart or pregnancy-related conditions, so ask your pharmacist or GP what is appropriate for you (healthdirect). Avoid using pain relief simply to mask symptoms and push through another hard match.

Rebuild forearm capacity gradually

Once daily gripping is settling, gentle resistance can be introduced. Clinical guidance for lateral elbow pain supports progressive exercise for the wrist and forearm muscles, adjusted to the person’s irritability and function (Journal of Orthopaedic & Sports Physical Therapy, 2022). The aim is not to find the most painful exercise. It is to give the area a manageable amount of work and build from there.

A simple starting sequence may include:

  • Isometric wrist extension: rest the forearm on a table with the palm down, then gently resist the back of the hand with the other hand without allowing movement.
  • Slow wrist extension: support the forearm and slowly raise and lower a very light weight, keeping the movement controlled.
  • Comfortable grip work: squeeze a rolled towel softly, then release rather than holding a maximal squeeze.

Keep the effort modest at first. Mild discomfort may be acceptable during rehabilitation, but sharp pain, steadily increasing symptoms or a clear flare that carries into the next day suggests the load needs to be reduced. Exercise selection and dosage should be individualised if you are unsure of the diagnosis or cannot use the arm normally.

Change the tennis load, not just the elbow

Return with a shorter hit rather than going straight back to a long singles match. Start with easier rallies, allow recovery time and add serving or forceful backhands later if those movements were the main trigger. Sudden changes in repetitive load are recognised contributors to tennis elbow symptoms (The Lancet, 2015).

Check whether you are squeezing the handle harder than necessary. A grip that is the wrong size, old strings or a racquet setup that feels harsh may also be worth discussing with an experienced tennis coach or racquet technician. Technique matters too. Repeatedly contacting the ball late or relying heavily on the wrist can increase the work demanded of the forearm, so a coaching session may be more useful than repeatedly changing braces.

A forearm strap sometimes makes activity more comfortable, but it is not a substitute for sensible loading and rehabilitation. Corticosteroid injections may reduce symptoms in the short term, yet a randomised trial found less favourable longer-term outcomes and more recurrence than placebo injection, so the benefits and drawbacks should be discussed with a suitably qualified medical practitioner (JAMA, 2013).

When the elbow should be assessed

Arrange an assessment if the pain is worsening, keeps returning, interferes with work or sleep, or has not begun to improve despite modifying your activity. Examination usually considers the precise pain location, grip and wrist strength, elbow and neck movement, nerve symptoms and the loads that started the problem. Imaging is not routinely required for a typical presentation, although it may be considered if another injury is suspected or progress is not as expected (Mayo Clinic).

Seek prompt medical care after a significant fall or impact, or if there is obvious deformity, severe swelling, inability to move the elbow, marked weakness, spreading numbness, fever or a hot red joint. These features are not typical of straightforward tennis elbow and may need urgent investigation (Mayo Clinic).

Frequently asked questions

Should I stop playing tennis completely?

Not always. A short break from painful strokes may be sensible, while comfortable daily movement and other exercise can usually continue. Return with a brief, easy hit and increase the load only if symptoms remain manageable during play and afterwards.

How long does tennis elbow take to settle?

Recovery varies with symptom severity, duration and how much repetitive load the arm still receives. Tennis elbow can persist for months in some people, so a gradual plan is usually more realistic than expecting it to disappear after a few days (Mayo Clinic).

Should I stretch a painful elbow?

A gentle forearm stretch may feel comfortable, but forcing the wrist into a strong stretch can aggravate an irritable tendon. Stop if it causes sharp pain, tingling or a lasting increase in symptoms. Progressive strengthening generally has a more useful role than repeatedly stretching into pain (Journal of Orthopaedic & Sports Physical Therapy, 2022).

Can elbow pain come from my neck?

Yes. Nerve irritation or referred pain from the neck can sometimes be felt around the elbow and forearm. Neck pain, tingling, numbness or weakness makes an assessment particularly worthwhile because the management may differ from that of a local tendon problem.
