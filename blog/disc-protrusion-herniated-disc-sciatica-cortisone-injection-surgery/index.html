Disc Protrusion and Sciatica: Exercise, Injections and Surgery

Updated 10 October 2026 · Hornsby Chiropractor

A scan report mentioning a disc protrusion or herniated disc can sound alarming. It does not, on its own, tell you which treatment you need. This guide explains disc-related symptoms, exercise choices, injections and surgery, with links to research and public health guidance. Treatment decisions should take account of your symptoms, examination findings, function and preferences.

Warning signs that need emergency assessment

New difficulty passing urine or controlling your bladder or bowels, numbness around the genitals or bottom, or rapidly worsening leg weakness needs emergency medical assessment. Do not wait to see whether stretching helps or book a routine chiropractic appointment. Call Triple Zero (000) if you need an ambulance. Healthdirect's spinal disc guidance explains these warning signs.

Disc protrusion, herniation and sciatica

Spinal discs sit between the vertebrae and help absorb load. A herniation occurs when disc material bulges or extends beyond its usual boundary. It may irritate or press on a nearby nerve, but some disc changes produce no symptoms. Age-related changes on a scan are not automatically the cause of a person's pain (NHS: slipped disc).

Sciatica usually describes pain spreading from the lower back or buttock into a leg, sometimes with tingling, numbness or weakness. A disc herniation is one possible cause; spinal narrowing and other conditions can also affect nerves. The distribution of symptoms and examination of strength, sensation and reflexes help a clinician assess the problem (healthdirect: sciatica).

Why a scan is not the whole decision

Many uncomplicated episodes of lower back pain do not need an immediate scan. Imaging can be useful when there are warning signs or when the result is likely to change management. The Australian Low Back Pain Clinical Care Standard emphasises assessment and appropriate use of imaging.

A large-looking bulge with relatively mild symptoms and a smaller change with substantial nerve symptoms can require different decisions. Ask what a scan finding means in your particular case rather than deciding for or against surgery from its size or the pain score alone.

McKenzie extension and exercise choices

The McKenzie method is an individual approach guided by assessment, including how symptoms respond to movement. It is broader than simply telling everyone to bend backwards. Extension may be one option for some people, but maintaining an extended lower back is not a guaranteed cure and no one exercise suits all disc-related symptoms.

A 2023 Cochrane review of the McKenzie method found no clinically important benefit for acute or subacute non-specific lower back pain, with low or very low certainty evidence. That population is not the same as every patient with confirmed disc herniation or nerve-root pain, so the findings should not be stretched into a universal recommendation.

Ask a qualified clinician which movements fit your symptoms and how to progress. Stop an exercise and seek advice if it produces spreading leg pain, new numbness or weakness, or a marked sustained flare. Our lumbar disc management guide covers general activity, sitting and driving considerations.

What research on inflammation can and cannot tell us

Research has explored both mechanical pressure and inflammatory effects around nerve roots. For example, an Otani and colleagues animal study published in 1997 tracked nerve and disc changes after experimentally induced disc herniation in dogs.

Such work helps researchers investigate mechanisms. An animal experiment does not establish how quickly a particular person will recover, prove that an exercise repairs their disc, or determine whether they need a procedure. Human treatment decisions need evidence relevant to the patient's condition and a clinical assessment.

Cortisone and epidural steroid injections

An epidural injection places medicines near spinal nerves to reduce pain and inflammation. It can be discussed for selected cases of nerve-related pain, but it does not physically put a disc back in place. Healthdirect's epidural injection information describes the procedure, alternatives and possible complications.

A 2020 Cochrane review of epidural corticosteroid injections for sciatica found small average improvements in leg pain and disability in the short term compared with placebo. These average benefits may not be clinically important for everyone, and adverse-effect reporting in the trials was limited. Relief is not guaranteed and its duration varies.

Discuss the likely benefit, risks, alternatives and aftercare with the doctor performing the procedure. Tell them about your medicines, including blood thinners, and relevant medical conditions. Do not stop prescribed medicine without their instructions. Possible complications include infection, bleeding, headache or nerve injury, so a decision requires more than a severe pain score.

When surgery is considered

Most slipped discs improve without surgery. A surgical opinion may be appropriate when symptoms persist despite other treatment or when weakness or numbness is worsening (NHS: slipped disc treatment). Some urgent nerve-compression problems need immediate hospital assessment, as described above.

Pain severity matters, but so do nerve function, day-to-day limitations, the pattern of recovery and whether imaging findings match the symptoms. A GP or specialist can discuss whether an injection, surgery or continued non-surgical management is appropriate. Surgery has potential benefits and risks; a general failure percentage cannot tell an individual what their outcome will be.

Questions to take to your appointment

  • What findings support the diagnosis, and could something else explain the symptoms?
  • Which changes would mean I need urgent care?
  • What activity can I continue, and how should I adjust it if leg symptoms increase?
  • What would an injection or surgery aim to improve, and what are the alternatives and risks?
  • When should we review progress or consider a referral?
