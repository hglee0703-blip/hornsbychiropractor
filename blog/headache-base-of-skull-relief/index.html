Headache at the Base of the Skull: What Helps

24 September 2026 · Headaches & neck pain

You have been sitting at the computer or driving for a while, then notice an ache where your neck meets your skull. It may stay there, spread over the back of your head or creep towards one side. Neck tenderness can occur with common headache disorders, but the location alone does not identify the cause (healthdirect).

Most people want to know two things: whether it is serious and what they can safely do now. Both depend on the pattern of pain and whether anything else is happening alongside it.

What pain at the base of the skull may mean

A pressure-like ache with tight or tender neck and scalp muscles can fit a tension-type headache. These headaches commonly cause dull, pressing pain rather than a throbbing sensation, although individual symptoms vary (Mayo Clinic).

A cervicogenic headache is pain referred from structures in the neck. It is often associated with reduced neck movement and may worsen with certain neck positions, but examination is needed because its features can overlap with other headaches (NCBI Bookshelf).

Brief, sharp or electric pain travelling from the upper neck into the scalp may suggest irritation of an occipital nerve. Occipital neuralgia can also cause tenderness over the nerve, yet diagnosis requires more than matching symptoms from a list (NCBI Bookshelf). Migraine and other headache disorders may also involve neck discomfort, so avoid assuming that every base-of-skull headache is simply a tight muscle.

Check for warning signs before treating it yourself

Call triple zero or seek urgent medical care for a sudden, extremely severe headache, particularly if it peaks quickly. Urgent assessment is also warranted when headache follows a significant head injury or occurs with confusion, fainting, seizures, weakness, numbness, trouble speaking, loss of balance, fever, a stiff neck or new visual disturbance (healthdirect).

A new headache after age 50 deserves medical review, especially with scalp tenderness, jaw pain while chewing or changes in vision. Those symptoms can occur with giant cell arteritis, which needs prompt treatment to reduce the risk of serious complications (Mayo Clinic). Seek advice sooner if you are pregnant or recently gave birth, have cancer or reduced immunity, take blood-thinning medicine, or the headache is steadily becoming more frequent or intense.

Practical steps you can try today

If there are no warning signs and the pain is mild, start gently. You could try a warm shower, a wrapped heat pack or a cool pack, choosing whichever feels more comfortable. Heat, cold, relaxation and gentle massage are among the self-care measures used for tension-type headache (Mayo Clinic).

  • Move your neck slowly within a comfortable range rather than pushing into pain.
  • Change position regularly during desk work, reading or driving.
  • Relax your jaw and let your shoulders drop instead of holding them braced.
  • Take a short walk if you feel well enough, rather than remaining still for the whole day.
  • Do not forcefully twist or crack your neck, particularly after an injury or if movement causes dizziness, nausea, visual symptoms, tingling or weakness.

Paracetamol or an anti-inflammatory medicine may be suitable for some adults, but not everyone can take them safely. Check the packet and ask a pharmacist or GP if you have other medical conditions, take regular medicines, are pregnant or are unsure. Frequent use of headache medicine can itself contribute to medication-overuse headache, so recurring reliance on pain relief is a reason to arrange an assessment rather than repeatedly increasing the dose (Mayo Clinic).

If the headache keeps returning

Keep a brief record of when the pain starts, how long it lasts, where it travels and what other symptoms occur. Note sleep, prolonged screen work, exercise, medicines and whether coughing, movement or exertion changes the pain. A headache diary can help a health professional recognise patterns and guide further investigation (healthdirect).

An assessment may include questions about the headache pattern, a neurological screen, blood pressure and examination of neck movement and tenderness. Cervicogenic headache cannot be confirmed by one posture test or one sore spot, and other headache causes need to be considered (NCBI Bookshelf).

Care should match the likely cause. For a neck-related headache, this may involve advice, graded exercise and selected hands-on treatment rather than aggressive manipulation. Research suggests manual and exercise therapy may help some people with cervicogenic headache, although study quality and treatment approaches vary (PubMed). Suspected migraine, nerve pain, infection, vascular disease or another medical cause may require GP assessment, medication or referral.

Frequently asked questions

These common questions can help with day-to-day decisions, but they cannot replace an examination if the pain is severe, unusual or persistent.

Can poor posture cause pain at the base of the skull?

A sustained or awkward neck position may aggravate neck pain and a neck-related headache, but posture is rarely a complete diagnosis by itself. Changing position regularly and keeping screens at a comfortable height may be more practical than trying to hold one supposedly perfect posture all day (healthdirect).

Is it safe to massage the painful area?

Gentle massage may ease muscle tension for some people with a tension-type headache (Mayo Clinic). Stop if it increases the headache or causes dizziness, nausea, tingling, weakness or visual symptoms. Avoid firm pressure after an injury or over an area that is swollen or unusually tender.

Could my pillow be contributing?

A pillow that leaves your neck uncomfortable may aggravate symptoms, although there is no single pillow type that suits everybody. Aim for a comfortable position that does not force your head sharply forwards, backwards or sideways, and seek assessment if changing pillows does not settle recurring pain.

How long should I wait before seeing someone?

Seek urgent help immediately for warning signs such as a sudden severe headache, neurological symptoms, fever with neck stiffness or headache after significant injury (healthdirect). Otherwise, book an appointment if the pain keeps returning, is worsening, disrupts sleep or normal activity, or requires frequent pain medicine.
