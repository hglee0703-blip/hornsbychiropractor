Return to Running After an Ankle Sprain Safely

04 November 2026 · Andy Lee, Chiropractor · Running injuries

Your ankle feels comfortable walking to the shops, but the first few jogging steps still feel uncertain. That is a common point to reach during recovery. A safe return to running after an ankle sprain depends less on the calendar and more on how the ankle handles walking, hopping, balance and repeated loading.

Pain and swelling may settle before strength, confidence and control have fully returned. Return-to-sport guidance recommends considering pain, ankle movement and strength, confidence, balance and sport-specific performance rather than relying on time alone (PubMed: PAASS framework).

Return to running after an ankle sprain: readiness first

Before testing a run, you should be able to walk briskly on level ground without limping or developing increasing pain. Stairs should feel reasonably controlled, and everyday activity should not leave the ankle noticeably worse later that day or the following morning. These checks are not a formal clearance test, but they reflect the pain, function and sensorimotor factors used in return-to-sport decision-making (PubMed: PAASS framework).

Try standing on the affected leg near a wall or bench. Then compare slow calf raises on each side. If those are comfortable, test a few gentle two-legged hops, followed by small single-leg hops. Marked pain, apprehension, poor landing control or a feeling that the ankle may give way suggests it is not ready for running load yet. Clinical guidelines support assessing balance, movement, strength and functional performance after a lateral ankle sprain (PubMed: ankle sprain clinical practice guideline).

Rule out problems that need assessment

Do not use a trial run to test an ankle that remains very painful to bear weight on. Significant tenderness directly over the ankle bones, an inability to take four steps, or concern about a fracture warrants medical assessment; these features form part of the Ottawa ankle rules used to guide the need for X-rays (PubMed: Ottawa ankle rules).

Seek prompt care for visible deformity, severe or worsening pain and swelling, an open wound, or a foot that becomes numb, unusually pale or cold. Persistent instability, locking or pain that is not improving also deserves examination rather than repeated attempts to run through it (Mayo Clinic).

Use a gradual run-walk progression

Choose a flat, predictable surface for your first session. A local oval or smooth path is usually easier to judge than a rocky trail or a route with steep descents. Wear familiar shoes rather than changing footwear at the same time as you restart running.

One practical first session is a brisk walk to warm up, followed by six rounds of one minute of easy jogging and two minutes of walking. Keep the pace conversational. This is an example, not a universal prescription. Shorten the jogging intervals if the ankle feels hesitant, or begin with fast walking if hopping is not yet comfortable.

During the session, watch for a developing limp, increasing pain, loss of push-off or a sense that the ankle is unstable. Check it again later and the next morning. A useful clinical rule is that symptoms should remain mild, your running pattern should stay normal, and the ankle should return to its pre-session state by the following day. This symptom response is a practical guide rather than a validated pass-or-fail test; return decisions should draw on several physical and psychological factors (PubMed: PAASS framework).

If the response is settled, repeat the session before changing it. Then increase one element at a time, such as total jogging time or pace. Hills, faster running and uneven trails can wait. If back discomfort appears as your running volume rises, the guide to lower back pain when running covers a few related load and technique considerations.

Rebuild strength, balance and confidence

Running exposes the ankle to repeated loading, so jogging alone is not always enough rehabilitation. Exercise-based treatment after a lateral ankle sprain commonly includes ankle movement, resistance work, balance training and progressively harder functional tasks (PubMed: ankle sprain clinical practice guideline).

A simple home routine may include:

  • slow calf raises, beginning with both legs and progressing towards one leg
  • ankle eversion against a light resistance band
  • single-leg balance beside a stable support
  • small hops forwards, sideways and on the spot once basic hopping is comfortable
  • controlled squats or step-downs to practise alignment through the hip, knee and ankle

Use a range and resistance you can control without a sharp increase in pain. Progress from steady movements to quicker changes of direction only after landings feel reliable. Hip mobility can also affect how some runners organise their stride, although it should not distract from direct ankle rehabilitation; this guide explains how to stretch tight hip flexors without forcing the movement.

Should you tape or brace the ankle?

An ankle brace or sports tape may provide useful external support during the return to activity, particularly if the ankle has been sprained before. Clinical guidelines support bracing and balance-focused exercise as options for reducing the risk of another lateral ankle sprain, with the choice guided by the activity and the individual (PubMed: ankle sprain clinical practice guideline).

Support is not a substitute for restoring strength, balance and running tolerance. It should fit comfortably, without causing numbness, rubbing or changes in foot colour. If you are unsure why the ankle remains painful or unstable, an assessment can help clarify what is limiting progress and whether referral or imaging is appropriate. You can read about what an appointment involves before deciding whether that would be useful.

Frequently asked questions

How long should I wait before running after an ankle sprain?

There is no single waiting period that suits every sprain. Severity, previous injury, walking comfort, strength, balance and hopping ability all matter, so function is more useful than choosing a date alone (PubMed: PAASS framework).

Can I run if the ankle is still slightly swollen?

A small amount of residual swelling does not answer the question by itself. Avoid running if swelling is increasing, weight-bearing is painful, movement is restricted or the ankle cannot manage brisk walking and hopping with reasonable control; pain and ankle function should both be considered before returning to sport (PubMed: PAASS framework).

Should I wear an ankle brace for my first runs?

A brace may be helpful, especially after a previous sprain or during activities with frequent direction changes. It should be combined with progressive strength and balance work rather than used as the only form of rehabilitation (PubMed: ankle sprain clinical practice guideline).

When can I return to trail running?

Start with flat, predictable running and make sure the ankle tolerates repeated hops and controlled changes of direction. Add gentle, familiar trails before technical ground or steep descents, because return-to-sport assessment should include tasks that reflect the demands of the chosen activity (PubMed: PAASS framework).
