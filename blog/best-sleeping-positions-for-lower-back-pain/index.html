Best Sleeping Positions for Lower Back Pain | Hornsby Chiro

26 August 2026 · Updated 10 October 2026 · Lower back pain

Disclaimer: The information provided in this article is for general educational purposes only and does not substitute for professional medical advice, diagnosis, or personalized care. Always consult a qualified healthcare professional regarding any specific spinal condition.

Waking with lower back stiffness can make those first steps uncomfortable. A different pillow arrangement or sleeping position may help you rest more comfortably, but morning pain does not reveal its cause by itself. This guide offers simple options to try and signs that need assessment.

A sleeping position may feel comfortable one night and less comfortable during a flare-up. Rather than trying to hold one supposedly perfect posture all night, look for a position that lets you rest without steadily increasing symptoms.

Why your sleeping posture matters for lower back pain

Sleep and pain can affect one another. A pillow or position change can be a useful comfort measure, but it does not prove that a disc is damaged or repair a specific spinal problem. Persistent pain that disrupts sleep deserves assessment (healthdirect).

For side sleeping, Mayo Clinic suggests drawing the legs up slightly and placing a pillow between the legs. For back sleeping, a pillow under the knees may help relax the back muscles and support the lower back curve (Mayo Clinic). Choose a position that feels comfortable rather than assuming one setup suits everyone.

Start with small adjustments using pillows you already have. You do not need to buy a new mattress before trying a more comfortable setup.

Sleeping on your side: Comfortable support

If side sleeping feels comfortable, try a pillow between your legs so the upper leg has support. Change the pillow thickness or position if it makes symptoms worse. A leg resting forward does not establish that the pelvis is out of alignment.

Some people find a pillow between the knees more comfortable. A sleeping position does not establish the cause of leg symptoms; seek assessment if these persist or worsen. If your symptoms involve sharp radiating pain down the leg, you might want to read our detailed guide on disc protrusion, sciatica, and non-surgical care options.

Let your knees bend a little if that feels comfortable. Avoid forcing yourself into a tightly curled position or holding a posture that increases pain.

Sleeping on your back: Supporting the lumbar curve

Back sleeping is another option to try if lying on your side is uncomfortable. There is no single sleeping position that suits every person with lower back pain.

A pillow under your knees may help relax your back muscles and support the lower back curve. Mayo Clinic also suggests trying a small rolled towel under the waist if more support is needed. Use only what feels comfortable (Mayo Clinic).

If you are managing a diagnosed disc problem or flare-up, discuss suitable positions and activity with your clinician. Our guide to lumbar disc injury management covers general activity and assessment considerations.

If you prefer sleeping on your stomach

Stomach sleeping can be uncomfortable for some people with back pain. If it aggravates your symptoms, try a different position rather than assuming that sleeping on your front has damaged your spine.

If you prefer sleeping on your stomach, a pillow beneath the hips and lower abdomen may ease strain. Mayo Clinic suggests testing whether a head pillow helps; leave it out if it strains your back (Mayo Clinic).

Change one part of the setup at a time so you can notice whether it helps. Do not persist with a position that steadily increases back or leg symptoms.

Choosing the right mattress and pillow setup

An extra-firm mattress is not automatically the best choice for lower back pain. Before replacing a bed, consider whether it is comfortable, supports you without obvious sagging and allows you to change position easily.

A randomised trial compared firm and medium-firm mattresses in 313 adults with chronic non-specific lower back pain and no referred pain. After 90 days, the medium-firm group had better outcomes for pain in bed and disability (Kovacs and colleagues, 2003). This does not prove that one firmness suits every condition or every sleeper. Individual comfort still matters.

Choose a head pillow that feels comfortable and supports your neck without forcing your head far forward or sideways. Reassess the setup if it brings on neck symptoms; one pillow shape does not suit everyone.

Frequently asked questions

Should I buy a firm mattress if I have lower back pain?

Not necessarily. A trial in adults with chronic non-specific lower back pain found better outcomes with medium-firm than firm mattresses (Kovacs and colleagues, 2003). Choose a comfortable, supportive option rather than assuming the hardest mattress will help everyone.

How long does it take to adapt to a new sleeping position?

There is no established adjustment period that applies to everyone. Try a comfortable change and judge it by sleep and symptoms rather than a deadline. You do not need to hold perfectly still all night or persist with a setup that makes pain worse.

Why is my lower back pain worse first thing in the morning?

Morning pain can have several causes, and the timing alone does not identify a disc or posture problem. Arrange an assessment if it persists, worsens or disrupts daily activity or sleep (healthdirect). Our guide to morning lower back pain discusses different patterns.

When sleeping changes are not enough

Seek medical advice if pain is worsening, repeatedly interrupts sleep or limits normal activity. New bladder or bowel changes, numbness around the genitals or bottom, or rapidly increasing leg weakness needs emergency assessment. Do not wait for a pillow or mattress change to help. In Australia, call Triple Zero (000) if you need an ambulance. See NHS back pain guidance for warning signs.
