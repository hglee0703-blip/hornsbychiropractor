Best Sleeping Positions for Lower Back Pain | Hornsby Chiro

26 August 2026 · Lower back pain

Disclaimer: The information provided in this article is for general educational purposes only and does not substitute for professional medical advice, diagnosis, or personalized care. Always consult a qualified healthcare professional regarding any specific spinal condition.

Every single week in my Hornsby clinic, I speak with people who wake up feeling like they are eighty years old. They swing their legs out of bed, brace themselves on the bedside table, and wince through those first ten steps. If that sounds familiar, you are not alone. Morning lower back stiffness is one of the most common complaints I see in local patients across the North Shore.

We spend roughly a third of our lives resting in bed. When you sleep in a position that twists or sags your spine, your back muscles remain tense for hours trying to compensate. Over time, poor sleep posture can aggravate existing spinal issues or create fresh inflammation in the spinal joints and surrounding discs.

Why your sleeping posture matters for lower back pain

When you sit or stand during the day, gravity exerts constant vertical compression on your spine. Night-time ought to be the period when your intervertebral discs hydrate and recover from daily mechanical strain. However, if your spine is twisted or bent at an awkward angle for seven or eight hours, those delicate spinal structures never get a break.

Research shows that improper sleeping alignment increases mechanical stress on the lumbar tissues (Desouzart et al., 2019). This ongoing strain can irritate nearby spinal nerves and lead to localized inflammation. Australian health guidelines emphasize that maintaining a natural spinal curve during rest is a key component of managing long-term mechanical back discomfort (Healthdirect, 2024).

Fixing your posture while asleep is not about buying an expensive bed overnight. It starts with simple body alignment adjustments using pillows you already own.

Sleeping on your side: The golden rules

Side sleeping is the most popular sleeping position, and for good reason. It works remarkably well for relieving pressure on the spinal joints, provided you keep your pelvis neutral. When you lie on your side, your top leg tends to drop forward. This simple drop twists your lower lumbar spine and rotates your pelvis out of alignment, putting asymmetric tension on the lower back muscles.

To fix this, place a firm pillow between your knees. The pillow keeps your hips, pelvis, and lumbar spine stacked in alignment throughout the night. If you suffer from nerve irritation or pain down your leg, this position can significantly reduce nerve stretching and swelling. If your symptoms involve sharp radiating pain down the leg, you might want to read our detailed guide on disc protrusion, sciatica, and non-surgical care options.

Draw your knees up slightly toward your chest in a gentle fetal pose. Avoid tucking into a tight ball, as excessive rounding flattens the natural curve of your lumbar spine and compresses the front of your spinal discs.

Sleeping on your back: Supporting the lumbar curve

Back sleeping is considered by many spinal specialists to be the most balanced position for distributing body weight evenly across your joints. When lying flat on your back, your weight is spread over a broad surface area, reducing specific pressure points along the neck and lumbar region.

However, lying completely flat on a firm mattress can cause your lower back to arch excessively, tightening the hip flexors and loading the facet joints. Place a small pillow or rolled towel directly underneath your knees. Flexing the knees slightly flattens the lumbar curve back into a neutral, relaxed position (Mayo Clinic, 2023).

For patients dealing with acute disc injuries, supporting the knees can relieve direct pressure on the lower back. If you are actively managing a disc herniation or flare-up, review our practical guide on lumbar disc injury management and daily posture strategies.

Is stomach sleeping ruining your spine?

I usually tell my patients to avoid stomach sleeping if they are dealing with active back or neck pain. Lying on your front forces your neck to turn sharply to one side for hours, which stresses the cervical facet joints. At the same time, stomach sleeping sinks your midsection into the mattress, causing your lower back to extend into an exaggerated arch.

This forced extension puts continuous compression on the posterior lumbar joints. According to public health resources on back care, stomach sleeping is the hardest position on your spinal alignment (Better Health Channel, 2024).

If you find it impossible to sleep any other way, place a thin pillow directly underneath your lower abdomen and hips. This prevents your pelvis from sinking too deep and relieves excessive arching in your lower back. Try using a thin head pillow, or skip head support entirely, to keep your neck closer to neutral.

Choosing the right mattress and pillow setup

People frequently ask me in Hornsby whether they should throw away their mattress and buy an ultra-firm orthopaedic bed. The short answer is usually no. For decades, traditional advice recommended sleeping on a mattress that felt as hard as a floor board. Modern clinical trials have disproven this myth.

A systematic review evaluating mattress firmness found that medium-firm mattresses consistently produce superior outcomes for reducing back pain and improving sleep quality compared to extra-firm options (Coto et al., 2015). A mattress that is too hard pushes against your hips and shoulders without supporting your waist. A mattress that is too soft lets your spine drop like a hammock.

Your pillow height matters just as much. When sleeping on your side, your pillow should fill the gap between the bed and your neck to keep your head level with your spine. When sleeping on your back, use a thinner contoured pillow so your head is not pushed forward toward your chest.

Frequently asked questions

Should I buy a firm mattress if I have lower back pain?

Not necessarily. Clinical studies show that medium-firm mattresses are generally best for managing lower back pain (Coto et al., 2015). An extra-firm mattress can create painful pressure points around your shoulders and hips, throwing your spine out of alignment.

How long does it take to adapt to a new sleeping position?

Changing long-standing sleep habits takes time and patience. Most people require about two to four weeks of consistent effort to feel comfortable in a new sleeping position. If you roll back to your old habit during sleep, do not worry; simply readjust when you wake up.

Why is my lower back pain worse first thing in the morning?

During the night, your spinal discs absorb fluid and swell slightly, increasing internal disc pressure. If you also sleep in a position that strains your joints, your surrounding back muscles tighten up to guard the area, causing noticeable stiffness upon waking (Healthdirect, 2024).
