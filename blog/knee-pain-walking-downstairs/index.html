Knee Pain Walking Downstairs: What Can Help

16 October 2026 · Andy Lee, Chiropractor · Knee pain

Knee pain walking downstairs can turn a routine trip to the kitchen, station platform or car park into something you approach cautiously. A useful starting point is to reduce the immediate irritation, change how you use the stairs for a while, then gradually rebuild the strength needed to lower your body with control.

Pain at the front of the knee or around the kneecap is often aggravated by stairs in people with patellofemoral pain, although stairs alone cannot tell you exactly what is causing the problem (Mayo Clinic). The right approach depends on whether symptoms came on gradually, followed an injury, or are accompanied by swelling, locking or instability.

Why knee pain walking downstairs happens

Walking down requires you to bend the supporting knee while your thigh muscles control the lowering movement. If the knee is irritated or has lost some strength, that controlled descent may feel worse than level walking. Pain around the kneecap is one possibility. Knee osteoarthritis can also cause pain, stiffness and reduced movement, while a meniscus injury may follow a twist and produce swelling, catching or locking (healthdirect; Mayo Clinic).

Location offers clues, but it is not a diagnosis. Pain below the kneecap, on the inner or outer joint line, or at the back of the knee may have different explanations. The timing matters too. Note whether the problem began after running, squats, gardening, a long bushwalk, a fall or a sudden increase in stair use.

Settle the knee before testing it

For the next few days, reduce unnecessary stair trips and use a lift or ramp where one is available. This is not the same as avoiding all movement. Keep up comfortable walking on flatter ground if you can do so without a marked increase in symptoms. General advice for knee pain includes modifying aggravating activity and using cold packs for short periods when appropriate (healthdirect).

On unavoidable stairs, hold the rail and move slowly. Putting more of your foot on the tread can feel steadier than balancing on the forefoot. If alternating steps is painful, take one step at a time. When going down, place the sore leg onto the lower step first, then bring the other leg down beside it. This allows the less painful side to do more of the controlled lowering.

A wrapped cold pack may be used after activity if the knee feels hot or irritated. Do not place ice directly on the skin. If you are considering pain medicine, ask a pharmacist or GP what is suitable, particularly if you take other medicines or have kidney, stomach, heart, blood pressure or bleeding concerns.

Build strength for the lowering movement

Exercise is commonly used for both patellofemoral pain and knee osteoarthritis. Research supports exercise targeting the hip and knee for patellofemoral pain, while a Cochrane review found land-based exercise can improve pain and physical function in knee osteoarthritis (PubMed; PubMed). The exercise still needs to match your current capacity.

Start with a stable bench, kitchen counter or rail nearby. These are reasonable options if there has been no significant injury and the knee is not giving way:

  • Sit-to-stand: rise from a firm chair and sit back down slowly. Use your hands or a higher seat if needed.
  • Supported mini squat: hold a counter, bend both knees a small amount, then stand. Keep the movement comfortable rather than chasing depth.
  • Low step-down: stand on a low step while holding a rail. Slowly lower the opposite heel towards the floor, then return. Begin with a very small range.
  • Bridge: lie on your back with knees bent, lift your hips comfortably, then lower with control.

One easy set of six to eight repetitions may be enough at first. Use a smaller movement or stop if you develop sharp pain, increasing swelling, catching or a sense that the knee will buckle. A practical guide is to choose a level that does not leave the knee clearly worse later that day or the following morning.

Adjust your stair technique without becoming rigid

Try to keep the kneecap roughly facing in the same direction as the middle toes rather than allowing the knee to drop markedly inwards. Leaning the trunk slightly forward from the hips may also make the descent feel more controlled. These are options to test, not rules that everyone must follow.

Footwear should feel secure on the stair and should not slip at the heel. Avoid carrying bulky loads while the knee is unreliable because that removes your view of the steps and may stop you using the rail. If pain on stairs is happening alongside discomfort during longer walks, the article on lower back pain when walking covers a few related pacing ideas.

When the knee should be assessed

Seek prompt medical care after a significant injury if you cannot bear weight, the knee looks deformed, it swells suddenly, or it is locked and cannot fully bend or straighten. A hot, red or very swollen knee, particularly with fever or feeling unwell, also needs urgent assessment (Mayo Clinic).

Arrange a non-urgent assessment if symptoms keep returning, are getting worse, disturb sleep, or have not begun to improve despite sensible activity changes. Giving way, repeated catching and persistent swelling are also reasons to have the knee examined. An assessment may include the knee, hip, ankle, walking pattern and the tasks that trigger pain. Imaging is sometimes useful, but the need for it depends on the history and examination rather than pain on stairs alone (healthdirect).

Frequently asked questions

Why does my knee hurt downstairs but not upstairs?

Going down requires the supporting leg to control your body as the knee bends. That can provoke an irritated knee even when climbing or flat walking feels manageable. Pain around the kneecap is commonly aggravated by stair use, but an examination may be needed if the pattern persists (Mayo Clinic).

Should I keep walking if my knee hurts?

Comfortable walking on level ground may be reasonable if the pain remains mild and the knee does not become more swollen or unstable. Shorten the distance or take a flatter route if symptoms build. Stop and seek assessment if you cannot bear weight, the knee locks, or it repeatedly gives way.

Will a knee brace or taping help on stairs?

Taping may be used alongside exercise and other treatment for some people with patellofemoral pain, but it is not considered a stand-alone answer (PubMed). A brace should fit properly and suit the likely cause, so ask a clinician if you are unsure which type, if any, is appropriate.

Do I need a scan for pain on stairs?

Not necessarily. A clinician can often begin by asking about the onset, checking movement and examining the knee. Imaging may be considered after trauma, when certain examination findings are present, or when symptoms do not improve as expected (healthdirect).
