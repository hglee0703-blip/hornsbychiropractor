Hip Pain Lying on Side at Night: Practical Help

09 October 2026 · Andy Lee, Chiropractor · Hip pain

Hip pain lying on side at night often feels like a deep ache over the outside of the hip, right where it presses into the mattress. It may settle when you roll over, only to start on the other side. The usual explanation is irritation of the tendons and soft tissues around the outer hip, although pain can also come from the hip joint or lower back.

The location of the pain, what brings it on during the day and whether there are symptoms below the knee all help narrow it down. Night pain on its own does not give us a diagnosis.

Why hip pain lying on side can feel worse

The bony point at the outside of your hip is called the greater trochanter. Several gluteal tendons attach nearby, with fluid-filled bursae helping reduce friction between tissues. Pain arising from this region is commonly grouped under the term greater trochanteric pain syndrome, or GTPS (NCBI Bookshelf).

Lying directly on the sore side places pressure over that area. Lying on the opposite side can also be uncomfortable if the top leg drops across the body, because this position compresses the outer-hip tendons against the underlying bone (BMJ clinical trial via PubMed, 2018). This helps explain why simply changing sides is not always enough.

Pain may be more noticeable in bed because there are fewer distractions and you stay in one position for longer. That does not necessarily mean the hip is being damaged during the night.

Common sources of pain around the hip

Gluteal tendinopathy and GTPS are common considerations when pain sits over the outer point of the hip. Typical aggravating activities can include side sleeping, climbing stairs, walking uphill, standing with weight shifted onto one leg and getting up from a low chair (NCBI Bookshelf). The area may also be tender when pressed.

Hip osteoarthritis more often causes pain around the groin or front of the thigh, with stiffness and reduced movement, although symptoms can also be felt at the side of the hip. Osteoarthritis symptoms vary considerably and an X-ray result does not always match how much pain or disability a person has (healthdirect).

Referred pain from the lower back is another possibility. Irritation involving a spinal nerve can produce pain, tingling, numbness or weakness through the buttock and leg, while other lower-back problems may refer an ache towards the hip without nerve symptoms (healthdirect). If turning in bed also triggers back pain, this article on lower back pain when rolling over may be useful.

What to change in bed tonight

Start by reducing direct pressure. If only one hip hurts, try lying on the other side with a firm pillow between your knees and ankles. The pillow should be thick enough to stop the upper thigh dropping sharply towards the mattress. Keeping the hips more evenly stacked may reduce compression around the painful outer hip, a principle used in education programmes for gluteal tendinopathy (BMJ clinical trial via PubMed, 2018).

Another option is sleeping on your back with a pillow beneath the knees. If you tend to roll towards the sore side, a pillow placed behind your back can act as a reminder rather than a rigid barrier. A very soft mattress may let the pelvis sag, while an extremely firm surface can increase local pressure, so comfort and support matter more than chasing a particular mattress label.

Avoid repeatedly digging into the tender point with a hard massage ball before bed. Gentle warmth may feel soothing, while a wrapped cold pack can suit a recently irritated area. Protect your skin and keep either approach brief; heat and cold can cause burns or tissue injury if they are too intense or applied directly (healthdirect).

What to do during the day

Think about recent changes rather than assuming the hip has suddenly worn out. A jump in walking distance, more hills, repeated stairs, a new gym routine or long periods standing with the weight on one leg can irritate load-sensitive tissues around the hip. Temporarily reducing the clearest aggravating activity is usually more sensible than stopping all movement.

Exercise is commonly used in managing gluteal tendinopathy. A clinical trial found that education about tendon loading combined with a targeted exercise programme performed better than a wait-and-see approach for important patient outcomes, although the right exercises and pace vary between people (BMJ clinical trial via PubMed, 2018). Early exercises should not repeatedly provoke a strong or lasting flare.

Stretching is not automatically the answer. Strong stretches that pull the sore leg across the body may increase compression at the outer hip. If stiffness is mainly felt at the front of the hip after prolonged sitting, use a controlled approach such as the options in this guide to stretching tight hip flexors, and stop if it reproduces sharp lateral hip pain.

When an assessment makes sense

Arrange an assessment if the pain keeps disturbing sleep, is worsening, limits normal walking or has not started settling after you modify the obvious triggers. A clinician may examine the hip and lower back, check strength and movement, and look for signs that the pain is referred. Scans are not always required at the first visit for typical outer-hip pain, because diagnosis is often based on the history and physical examination (NCBI Bookshelf).

Seek urgent medical care after a significant fall if you cannot stand or put weight through the leg, particularly if the leg appears shortened or turned out, as these can occur with a hip fracture (healthdirect). Prompt care is also needed for a hot, swollen joint with fever or feeling acutely unwell, because joint infection requires urgent treatment (healthdirect).

New loss of bladder or bowel control, numbness around the groin or rapidly increasing leg weakness requires emergency assessment because these may be signs of serious nerve compression (healthdirect). Frequently asked questions

Should I sleep on the painful hip?

If direct pressure clearly increases the pain, it is reasonable to avoid sleeping on that side for now. Try the other side with a pillow supporting both the knee and ankle, or lie on your back. The aim is a tolerable position, not staying perfectly still all night.

Is side-of-hip pain always bursitis?

No. The term bursitis is often used casually, but outer-hip pain may involve the gluteal tendons, bursae or both and is commonly described as greater trochanteric pain syndrome (NCBI Bookshelf). Pain from the hip joint or lower back can sometimes feel similar.

Will a pillow between my knees help?

It may help if it stops the upper leg falling across the body and eases pressure around the outer hip. Support the ankle as well as the knee so the whole leg rests comfortably. If the position increases groin, back or leg pain, change it.

How long should I wait before having it checked?

Consider an assessment if sleep is repeatedly affected, walking is becoming difficult or symptoms continue despite changing pressure and activity. Seek care sooner after an injury or if you develop fever, marked swelling, progressive weakness or new bladder, bowel or saddle-area symptoms (healthdirect).
