What Does a Chiropractor Do? Your First Visit Explained

10 October 2026 · Andy Lee, Chiropractor · Chiropractic appointments

Before booking for a sore back or stiff neck, you want to know what the visit actually involves. What does a chiropractor do, and will you have a say in treatment? A consultation usually includes questions about your health and an examination of your muscles, bones and joints. The findings help guide the discussion about care or referral (healthdirect).

What does a chiropractor do?

A chiropractor is an allied health professional who assesses and manages problems involving muscles, bones and joints. Chiropractic care may include joint manipulation or mobilisation, soft-tissue techniques, exercise advice and changes to daily activities. Chiropractors are not medical doctors, and a GP can help assess whether this type of care is appropriate for your condition (healthdirect).

The useful question is which approach fits your particular concern. Ask what the assessment suggests, how confident the clinician is about it, and what another option would involve. A general description of a profession cannot tell you which treatment you need.

What happens at a first chiropractic appointment?

At Hornsby Chiropractor, the consultation starts with your symptoms, relevant history and the activities that matter to you. You might want to sit through a work meeting, return to gardening or understand why turning your head is uncomfortable. Describe the task in your own words rather than trying to supply a diagnosis. Our consultation and care information explains how findings and suitable options are discussed.

A first visit includes a health history and physical examination. The examination depends on the problem being assessed (Mayo Clinic). For a new episode of low back pain, Australian guidance recommends checking the symptom history, movement, daily function and signs that could point to a serious cause; a focused nerve examination may be appropriate when nerve involvement is suspected (Low Back Pain Clinical Care Standard).

Tell the clinician if a movement is uncomfortable or if you do not understand why a test is being suggested. You can ask for an explanation before continuing. Ask which findings are clear and what remains uncertain, so you leave with an understandable account of the next step.

Discuss the options before treatment

Decisions about care should include understandable information, time for questions and informed consent before examination or treatment. Ahpra's shared Code of conduct applies to chiropractors and also requires discussion of fees and the level of care proposed (Ahpra Code of conduct).

Ask why a particular technique is suggested, what evidence supports it for your problem, its expected benefits and risks, and the alternatives. Write down what you want to be able to do, then ask how progress would be reviewed. You can decline or withdraw consent to treatment (NSW Health informed consent information).

Hands-on treatment carries risks. Some people develop temporary soreness, headaches or tiredness after a spinal adjustment; serious complications have also been reported, including nerve problems and a type of stroke following neck manipulation (Mayo Clinic). Ask about risks relevant to your health and the proposed technique. Share your concerns before deciding.

Will you need scans or medical review?

An X-ray or MRI is not an automatic part of a first visit for low back pain. Australian guidance recommends imaging when there is a clinical reason, such as concern about a serious underlying cause. A scan can show changes that also occur in people without pain, so findings need to be explained alongside your history and examination (Low Back Pain Clinical Care Standard).

Ask what a proposed scan would help decide. If you already have relevant imaging or reports, bring them rather than arranging a new scan yourself. When low back symptoms remain disabling, worsen or develop concerning features, review and referral may be needed (Australian guidance on review and referral).

Some symptoms need urgent medical care. Back pain with loss of limb feeling or movement, or changes in bladder or bowel control, requires emergency help: call 000. Significant injury, severe rapidly developing pain, or fever and feeling unwell with back pain also need urgent medical assessment (healthdirect back pain guidance). Do not wait for a routine clinic appointment in these situations.

How to prepare for your first visit

Write a short note about when symptoms began, what changes them, and what they stop you doing. Bring a list of medicines and supplements, allergies, relevant medical history, and existing test results or referral letters. These are useful preparation steps for healthcare appointments generally (Better Health Channel).

Include the questions you most want answered. For example: what are we trying to improve, what should I do between visits, and when would we reconsider the plan? If your concern relates to a desk or another specific activity, a photo or brief description of that setup can give the discussion a practical starting point. For desk-related symptoms, our guide to neck discomfort during computer work can help you think about the details to mention.

Before attending, confirm the appointment type, fee and any paperwork with the clinic. If you need communication or access support, ask about arrangements when booking. A support person and a written question list can help you remember the discussion (Better Health Channel appointment preparation).

Frequently asked questions

Is a chiropractor a medical doctor?

No. Chiropractors are allied health professionals, not medical doctors (healthdirect). Ask which profession is best suited to the concern you want assessed.

Do I have to have an adjustment?

You can discuss alternatives and choose whether to consent. You can also decline or withdraw consent to treatment (NSW Health). Raise any preferences or concerns before a technique is started.

How many appointments will I need?

Ask for an individual recommendation and a clear review point. Good professional practice includes discussing expected outcomes, fees and the proposed level of care before proceeding (Ahpra Code of conduct). Ask what would prompt a change to the plan.
